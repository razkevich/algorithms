Back-of-the-Envelope

QPS · storage · bandwidth · latency — recallable under interview pressure.

The point of estimation in a design interview isn't to be precise — it's to justify your architecture. "We need sharding" is a non-answer. "We have ~12K writes/sec at peak, that's beyond a single Postgres node, so we shard" is the answer. This page is the toolkit to get to that second sentence in 60 seconds.

Skip estimation if it doesn't fork the design.

Your interviewer's signal: do you know when numbers matter? URL Shortener at 100M writes/month doesn't change anything material — mention it, move on. Twitter at 500M tweets/day forces fanout choices — that one matters. Don't burn 5 of your 45 minutes on math the design doesn't need.

🧮 The 5-step ritual

Memorize this. Every estimation in every interview reduces to these five moves:

  1. Pick the load anchor. DAU, MAU, requests/sec, or items uploaded — pick the one the prompt gives you. (If none given, ask. "How many users?" is always a fair clarifying question.)
  2. Convert to per-second. Divide by 86,400 (s/day) or 2.5 × 10⁶ (s/month). That's your average QPS.
  3. Apply peak factor. Multiply average QPS by 2–3× for typical apps, 5–10× for spiky workloads (sports, news, sales). State the multiplier you used.
  4. Multiply by per-event size for storage / bandwidth. Don't forget metadata, indexes, replication factor (3× default).
  5. Project forward. Storage = current × (1 + growth)ⁿ over the design horizon. Often 5 years × 1.5× growth/year is the default ask.

Round aggressively.

Use 10⁶ not 1,048,576. Use 2.5M sec/month not 2,592,000. ≈ is your friend — interviewers care that you can reason about magnitude, not arithmetic. "Roughly 100GB" beats fumbled long division every time.

📐 Powers of 2 ↔ 10 — instant conversion

The conversion most people whiff under pressure. Memorize the four rows:

Bit-width Powers of 2 ≈ Powers of 10 Name What it sizes
10 bits1,02410³ (thousand)kiloKB, k-users in a small region
20 bits1,048,57610⁶ (million)megaMB, MAU of a niche product
30 bits1.07 × 10⁹10⁹ (billion)gigaGB, internet-scale users
40 bits1.10 × 10¹²10¹² (trillion)teraTB, 1 year of analytics events
50 bits1.13 × 10¹⁵10¹⁵ (quadrillion)petaPB, hyperscaler-tier data lake

Counter-trick: a 32-bit int = 4 bytes; max value ≈ 4 × 10⁹. A 64-bit long = 8 bytes; max ≈ 1.8 × 10¹⁹. Pick ID widths accordingly — if your system mints 1B IDs/day, 32-bit overflows in 4 days; 64-bit lasts forever.

The QPS cheat: divide by 100,000.

10⁵ seconds in a day is the magic number. 1B DAU × 1 action = 10K QPS avg. 100M DAU × 10 actions = 10K QPS avg. 10M DAU × 100 actions = 10K QPS avg. Once you internalize 10⁵, every QPS estimate becomes one division.

⚡ Latency ladder — the numbers every programmer should know

Times from L1 cache to cross-continent network, on a log scale. Each tick is 10× the previous. The right column scales them up so a CPU op = 1 second of human time — useful for explaining "why this network call dominates everything."

Why the human-time column matters: if your design has a hot path that does cache miss → SSD read → cross-region call, that's "1 day + 1 day + 5 years" of CPU time. Restate it in those terms when explaining a bottleneck — it's far more vivid than "150ms" to a non-systems interviewer.

🧪 Worked example — URL Shortener (Day 1's mock)

Prompt: A URL shortener. 100M new short URLs/month. Read:write ratio 100:1. Plan for 5 years.

Step 1 — load anchor
Writes: 100M / month. Reads: 100 × writes = 10B / month.
Step 2 — convert to QPS
Writes: 100M / 2.5×10⁶ s = 40 QPS avg.
Reads: 10B / 2.5×10⁶ s = 4,000 QPS avg.
Step 3 — peak factor (×3)
Peak writes ≈ 120 QPS. Peak reads ≈ 12,000 QPS.
Step 4 — storage / event size
Per row ≈ 500 B (short code + long URL + user_id + created_at).
Monthly: 100M × 500B = 50 GB.
5-year cumulative (no growth): 50 GB × 60 months = 3 TB.
With ×3 replication and ×1.5 index overhead: ~14 TB total.
Step 5 — what does this fork?
  • 120 writes/sec, 12K reads/sec → single Postgres handles writes, but reads need a cache.
  • 14 TB → well within sharded Postgres or DynamoDB; no exotic store needed.
  • Read-heavy 100:1 → Redis in front of DB, near-100% cache hit on hot keys.

That's the entire estimation justification: the math points at Postgres + Redis. No K8s magic, no sharding decisions to make on Day 1.

⚠️ Common pitfalls

Pitfalls that signal "junior" to a staff interviewer:

  • Ignoring peak. Capacity-planning to average QPS means you melt down at lunch hour. Always state your peak factor.
  • Forgetting replication. "We need 1 TB" — for one replica? Three? Six? State it.
  • One-pass write math. A "write" includes WAL, indexes, replication, often a cache invalidation, often a search-index update. Single logical write = 5–10 physical IOPS.
  • Conflating MAU and DAU. 1B MAU and 1B DAU are wildly different traffic profiles.
  • Hot-key blindness. "10K reads/sec" averaged across 100M keys is fine. "10K reads/sec all hitting Justin Bieber's row" is your outage.
  • Endless precision. 4-significant-figure arithmetic in your head, slowly, while the interviewer waits. Round to 1 sig-fig and move.

What an interviewer wants to hear in 90 seconds:

"Roughly 100M users, ~30M DAU at 50%, 5 actions/day each, that's 150M events/day, ~2K QPS average, peak ~6K. Each event is ~1KB so we're writing ~150GB/day, ~50TB/year — single Postgres can do this if we shard by user_id. Reads dominate at 100:1, so we'll front it with a Redis read-through cache. Let me draw the schema."

That's it. Three sentences of math, justified architecture choice. Move on.
