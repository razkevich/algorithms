§ 01  Redundancy & failover

Spare capacity is cheap; downtime is not.

Every production system is a bet against component failure. The interesting questions are not whether to be redundant but where, how synchronously, and at what latency cost. Active-active vs active-passive, multi-AZ vs multi-region, RTO vs RPO — these are the levers, and each has a price you pay every day, not just on the day of the outage.

Concept · The redundancy ladder Single AZ (a rack failure kills you) → multi-AZ (a fire in one DC is fine; a region-wide control-plane bug is not) → multi-region active-passive (DR-ready, replication lag is your RPO) → multi-region active-active (write-anywhere, conflict-resolution becomes your day job).

RTO = how long you’re down. RPO = how much data you lose. The four DR tiers map cleanly: backup & restore (hours/hours), pilot light (data replicated continuously, infra dormant), warm standby (smaller copy running), hot standby / multi-site active-active (zero-RTO, zero-RPO — if your CAP choice allows it). The right tier is the one where the cost of downtime exceeds the cost of redundancy — not the most expensive one your CTO read about.
Active-active vs active-passive vs N+1: when which?

Active-passive: one node serves traffic, the other is hot-standby; failover flips the role. Simple, no conflict resolution, but capacity sits idle and failover has a real RTO (seconds to minutes, depending on detection). The default for stateful systems where convergence is expensive (single-leader databases, classic Postgres HA via Patroni).

Active-active: both nodes serve traffic concurrently. No idle capacity, no failover step — but you need either stateless services or a way to resolve concurrent writes (CRDTs, last-writer-wins, app-level merge). Default for stateless API tiers, multi-region read fleets, and CRDT-friendly stores like DynamoDB Global Tables.

N+1 / N+M: capacity-planning shorthand. Run N nodes for the load + 1 (or M) for redundancy. N+1 survives one failure; N+2 survives a failure during a deploy; large fleets at scale want N+M where M scales with the failure-domain blast radius. AWS, GCP, and big SaaS shops typically design for losing an entire AZ (so N+M sized to one AZ's worth of capacity).

What the interviewer is probing: do you understand that capacity planning and redundancy are the same conversation? “We're active-active across two AZs” is incomplete — if losing one AZ leaves you at 100% load, you have no headroom for the recovery itself.
Multi-AZ vs multi-region: what does each cost you, and what does each buy?

Multi-AZ is cheap and almost free of consistency cost. Inter-AZ latency is sub-millisecond (typically 0.5–2 ms within a region); synchronous replication is viable. AWS, GCP, and Azure all charge negligibly for cross-AZ traffic compared to cross-region. You get protection from rack/DC failures and most hardware/power events.

Multi-region protects against region-wide failures (control-plane bugs, fiber cuts, regional storage outages, regulatory data-residency mandates). The cost ladder:

  • Latency: 50–200 ms cross-region. Synchronous quorum across regions is mostly out of the question for OLTP.
  • Bandwidth: egress charges; cross-region replication has real cost at scale.
  • Consistency: async replication gives you a non-zero RPO (seconds to minutes); active-active demands conflict resolution.
  • Operational complexity: regional control-plane partitions, asymmetric failures (one region partially up), DNS TTL games during failover.

Most products start multi-AZ and add multi-region only when the cost of a regional outage exceeds the engineering investment. Real region-wide AWS/GCP outages happen ~1–2 times a year; do the math.

Define RTO and RPO. What's the trick people miss?

RTO (Recovery Time Objective) = the maximum acceptable time the service is down. RPO (Recovery Point Objective) = the maximum acceptable amount of data loss, measured in time.

The trick: they’re not free to lower together. RPO=0 requires synchronous replication to a separate failure domain — which means every write pays the round-trip latency, every day. RTO=0 means active-active — which means you’re running (and paying for) both sides, plus the conflict-resolution machinery.

The other miss: RTO is dominated by detection, not recovery. If you detect a region failure in 90 seconds and DNS TTL is 60 seconds and clients cache it for 300 seconds, your RTO floor is ~7 minutes regardless of how fast your standby comes up. Spend on detection & client behavior before spending on warmer standbys.

Interview gotcha: “Our RTO is zero, RPO is zero, we’re multi-region active-active.” Then they ask: “On a network partition between regions, do you serve writes on both sides? What happens to consistency?” If you can’t answer this in CAP terms, your RPO=0 claim collapses.
Walk through the four DR tiers: backup, pilot light, warm standby, hot standby.

Backup & restore: snapshots stored cross-region; recovery rebuilds infra and restores. Cheapest. RTO: hours. RPO: depends on snapshot frequency (often 24h). Right for non-critical workloads.

Pilot light: minimal core (databases replicating, AMIs/images ready) running in DR; app tier scaled to zero. On disaster, scale up the app tier. RTO: 10s of minutes. RPO: replication lag (seconds). The sweet spot for many SaaS workloads.

Warm standby: a smaller-but-running copy of the full stack in DR; route traffic, then scale up. RTO: minutes. RPO: seconds. Good for tier-1 services where minutes of downtime are real money.

Hot standby / multi-site active-active: full capacity in both regions, traffic routed to both (or seamlessly routable). RTO: near zero. RPO: zero (sync) or seconds (async). Most expensive; mandatory for true real-time systems (payments, ad bidding, gaming matchmaking).

The picker: cost-of-downtime per minute × expected outage minutes per year > cost of the higher tier? Move up. Otherwise stay.

How do you actually trigger a failover? DNS vs LB vs anycast.

Three layers, three trade-offs:

  • DNS-based failover (Route 53 health checks, GCP Cloud DNS, Cloudflare): cheap, works across regions and providers. Cost: TTL caching. Even with TTL=60s, broken resolvers and OS caches stretch real propagation to many minutes. The MTBF-vs-MTTR tradeoff is poor for outage scenarios.
  • Load-balancer failover (cross-zone health checks, target-group draining): seconds, but the LB itself is the failure domain. Multi-AZ LB (ALB, NLB) is multi-AZ-resilient out of the box.
  • Anycast (the same IP advertised from multiple PoPs via BGP): hardware-fast, sub-second convergence. Used by CDNs (Cloudflare, Fastly), AWS Global Accelerator, GCP Global LB. The right answer for low-RTO global services, at the cost of complexity (BGP, route flaps).

Pattern in practice: anycast at the edge for clients, regional LBs internally, DNS only as a coarse last-resort failover. Don’t use DNS as your second-by-second steering mechanism.

What is split-brain and how do you prevent it?

Split-brain: a network partition leaves two halves of a cluster each thinking the other is dead. Both elect themselves leader; both accept writes; on heal, you have divergent state and no automatic merge. Catastrophic for stateful systems.

Prevention:

  • Quorum-based: majority required to elect (Raft, Paxos). With 5 nodes, a 2-3 partition has only one majority; the minority side refuses to lead. Always odd numbers — 4 nodes can split 2-2 with no majority.
  • Witness / arbiter: a third site (often a tiny tiebreaker VM) participates in quorum without holding data. Common in 2-DC active-active.
  • Fencing: the new leader actively kills the old one (STONITH — “shoot the other node in the head”) or revokes its access (storage fencing, IP failover). Mandatory when you can’t use quorum.
  • Lease-based: the leader holds a time-bounded lease; on expiry it stops serving without contacting the cluster. Combined with bounded clock drift, this prevents stale leaders from continuing to act.
Probing: “Why is split-brain prevention hard for two-node clusters?” The answer: there is no majority of two. You always need an external tiebreaker (witness, shared storage with fencing, or an external arbiter). This is why two-node HA is generally a bad pattern.
What is a fencing token, and why do you need one?

A fencing token is a monotonically increasing number issued by the lock service to each leader. Every action the leader takes against shared resources includes the token; resources reject actions with a stale (lower) token.

The motivating problem: a leader gets a lock, pauses (GC, hypervisor freeze, network blip), the lock expires, a new leader is elected, the old leader wakes up still believing it holds the lock, and writes. With no fencing, both writes succeed — the lock service’s “you don’t hold the lock anymore” arrives too late. With fencing, the storage layer sees token=11 then token=12 then token=11 again, and rejects the third.

This is Martin Kleppmann’s point against using Redis Redlock without fencing: Redlock can give you mutual exclusion in time intervals, but without fencing tokens, a paused client can still corrupt state. ZooKeeper, etcd, and well-implemented Chubby provide tokens (the zxid / mod-revision) for free.

Gotcha: if the storage layer doesn’t check the token, your fencing is theatrical. The storage must reject lower tokens — it’s an end-to-end property, not a lock-service property.
What is chaos engineering and why isn't it just “breaking things in production”?

Chaos engineering is the discipline of injecting controlled failures to verify that resilience hypotheses hold. Born at Netflix (Chaos Monkey, 2011); the explicit principle is the steady-state hypothesis: define what “healthy” looks like as a metric, inject failure, verify the metric stays in band.

The discipline part: (1) hypothesis up front, (2) smallest blast radius that proves the hypothesis, (3) abort criteria and a kill switch, (4) production where possible (staging is full of lies), (5) automation so it runs continuously, not as a quarterly party.

Modern tooling: Gremlin, Chaos Mesh (CNCF, K8s-native), Litmus, AWS FIS (Fault Injection Service), Toxiproxy (network-layer). GameDays are scheduled team exercises with realistic scenarios — the human side of the discipline.

Probing: “Have you ever run chaos in production?” The right answer mentions a hypothesis, a blast radius, a finding, and a fix. The wrong answer says “we don’t have time for that” — which means you’ll find the same failures during a real outage.
What are leases, and why are they better than locks for distributed systems?

A lease is a lock with a built-in expiration. The holder must actively refresh; if it dies or partitions, the lease expires and the resource is reclaimable without external intervention.

This solves the lock-with-dead-holder problem: a classic mutex held by a crashed process needs an out-of-band cleanup (someone notices, kills the lock). A lease just expires. Combined with fencing tokens, this is the foundation of every modern leader-election system: etcd leases, ZooKeeper ephemeral nodes, Chubby leases, GCP Spanner’s TrueTime-based leases.

The catch: lease duration trades availability for safety. Short leases mean fast failover but more refresh traffic and more risk of false-positive expiry under load. Typical values: 5–15 seconds for leadership leases, with a refresh at half the duration.

// etcd lease pattern (Go)
lease, _ := cli.Grant(ctx, 10) // 10s lease
cli.Put(ctx, "leader/svc-foo", nodeID, clientv3.WithLease(lease.ID))
keepAlive, _ := cli.KeepAlive(ctx, lease.ID)
// process consumes keepAlive channel; on stop, lease expires, key deletes
What is “blast radius” and how do you reduce it?

Blast radius = the scope of impact of a single failure. A bug in shared infrastructure has a wide blast radius (one bad config kills every tenant); a bug in a per-tenant deployment has a small one.

Reduction techniques, in order of architectural depth:

  • Cellular architecture / shuffle sharding: partition customers into independent cells; failures stay local. Used by AWS for Lambda, DynamoDB, S3 internals.
  • Bulkheads: isolate resources (thread pools, connection pools) per dependency so one slow downstream can’t starve another (see §03).
  • Canary deploys / gradual rollout: 1% → 10% → 50% → 100%, with auto-rollback on SLO regression.
  • Feature flags: ship code dark, enable per-cohort, kill instantly without a deploy.
  • Per-tenant quotas: one noisy neighbor can’t consume the cluster’s capacity.

The shift in the last decade: blast-radius reduction has gone from “nice operational hygiene” to “mandatory pre-launch design review item” at hyperscale shops.

Sync vs async replication: when which?

Sync replication: the primary waits for at least one replica to ack before confirming the write. RPO = 0 if you survive losing only the primary. Cost: every write pays the round-trip; if a replica is slow or partitioned, writes stall (or you reduce safety to recover).

Async replication: primary acks the client, replica catches up later. Latency stays primary-only; on primary loss, you lose whatever was in the replication lag. Default for most setups.

Quorum / semi-sync: wait for k of N replicas. The standard for distributed databases (Cassandra QUORUM, Postgres synchronous_commit=remote_write/remote_apply with multiple synchronous_standby_names). Bounds RPO without paying full sync cost.

The honest hybrid: sync within a region (cheap, low RPO) + async across regions (fast writes, accept regional RPO). Most production systems land here; it’s why “multi-region with RPO=0” is rare and expensive.

Gotcha: Postgres’ default is synchronous_commit=on — which is local sync (WAL flushed to local disk), not replica-sync. To get replica-sync, you need synchronous_standby_names set. Many teams discover this on the day they need it.
Why is “just keep retrying” not a redundancy strategy?

Retries assume transient failure with capacity available to handle the retry. In a real outage, neither holds. Three failure modes:

  • Retry storm: downstream slowdown causes upstream timeouts & retries; effective load grows; downstream gets even slower; collapse. Without back-off and jitter, retries actively hurt recovery.
  • Cascading saturation: retry budget on service A consumed by failures from service B; A starts failing requests it could have served; A’s callers retry; the failure spreads upward.
  • Idempotency violation: retries on non-idempotent operations cause duplicates (double-charge, double-message). Without idempotency keys, retry safety is a fiction.

Real redundancy is structural: more capacity, more failure domains, isolation. Retries are fault-tolerance polish on top of an already-redundant design, not a substitute for it. See §03 for the discipline of doing them correctly.

§ 02  Rate limiting

The polite way to say no.

Rate limiting is how a service protects its capacity from any caller (legitimate, abusive, or accidentally-DDoSing-itself) while preserving fairness across tenants. The interesting questions are not which algorithm — they’re where in the stack, shared or per-instance, and what the client is supposed to do when you say 429.

Concept · The five families Fixed window (counter per N-second bucket) — cheap, but allows 2x burst at boundaries. Sliding window log (timestamps in a queue) — accurate, but memory grows with rate. Sliding window counter (weighted blend of two adjacent windows) — the practical sweet spot. Token bucket (capacity = burst, refill rate = sustained) — lets bursts through, throttles sustained load. Leaky bucket (FIFO queue draining at fixed rate) — smooths bursts; used in network shaping (RFC 1122-era).

Token bucket is the modern default for APIs (AWS, Stripe, GitHub all use variants). Sliding window counter is what Cloudflare, NGINX, and Envoy use at the edge. Fixed window is what you implement on the back of an envelope and regret.
Compare token bucket vs leaky bucket. Which do most APIs use, and why?

Token bucket: a bucket holds up to capacity tokens; tokens are added at rate r; each request consumes one (or more, for weighted operations). If the bucket is empty, reject. Allows bursts up to bucket capacity, then settles to rate r.

Leaky bucket: requests enter a FIFO queue; the queue drains at constant rate r; if the queue is full, reject (or in some variants, delay). Smooths output to a constant rate; bursts are queued, not allowed through.

Most public APIs (AWS, Stripe, GitHub, Twitter) use token bucket because real client traffic is bursty by nature (page-load fan-out, batch jobs starting). Penalizing legitimate bursts is poor UX. Leaky bucket fits places where downstream must see smooth load — network egress shapers, payment processor uplinks, log forwarders feeding rate-limited destinations.

// Token bucket pseudocode
function allow(key, cost = 1) {
  now = Date.now()
  state = load(key)  // {tokens, last}
  state.tokens = min(capacity, state.tokens + (now - state.last) * rate)
  state.last = now
  if (state.tokens >= cost) { state.tokens -= cost; save(key, state); return true }
  return false
}
Why does fixed-window rate limiting allow 2x burst at boundaries?

Suppose limit = 100 req/min, fixed minute boundaries. A client sends 100 requests in the last second of minute T (all allowed) and 100 more in the first second of minute T+1 (all allowed, fresh window). In a 2-second span the client made 200 requests — double the nominal limit.

This is fine for protecting against sustained abuse but lets a coordinated attacker double their effective rate by aligning to window boundaries. Sliding window counter fixes it by interpolating: count = current_window_count + previous_window_count × (1 - elapsed_in_current/window_size). Cheap (two counters) and robust against boundary games. This is the algorithm you’ll see in Cloudflare’s rate-limiter and most production gateways.

Probing: “What do you do when memory matters and accuracy matters?” Sliding window counter is the answer — O(1) per key (two integers) and accuracy within a few percent. The interviewer wants to hear you reject the dumb-and-cheap option without overpaying for log-based exactness.
Where does rate limiting actually run: edge, gateway, or service?

All three, doing different jobs:

  • Edge / CDN / WAF (Cloudflare, Akamai, AWS Shield, AWS WAF): coarse, IP-based, DDoS-shaped. Job: drop traffic before it hits your network. The 99.9% of garbage that should never reach origin.
  • API gateway / ingress (Kong, Envoy, NGINX, AWS API Gateway, Apigee): per-API-key, per-route, per-tenant tier. Job: enforce contract limits with a single source of truth, return clean 429s with Retry-After.
  • Service-side: business-logic limits and concurrency caps (e.g., “at most 5 concurrent transcodes per user”). Job: protect specific resources where the gateway can’t see the cost.

The mistake is putting all enforcement in one layer. Edge stops volumetric abuse; gateway enforces contracts; service protects expensive operations. They compose; they don’t replace each other.

Centralized (Redis) vs decentralized (per-instance) rate limiting: trade-offs?

Centralized (Redis): one source of truth; every API node calls Redis to atomically increment-and-check (typically with INCR + EXPIRE, or a Lua script for atomicity). Strict accuracy, easy quota math. Cost: every request pays a Redis round-trip; Redis becomes a critical dependency; rate-limiter latency floor is Redis latency.

Decentralized: each instance enforces independently with local state. Zero extra latency, no shared dependency. Cost: total allowed requests = limit × instance_count, so you over-allocate by your fleet size. With sticky sessions or consistent hashing, partial accuracy is recoverable.

Hybrid (the production pattern): each instance gets a portion of the budget from a central counter, refreshed periodically. Stripe’s public design talks call this “rate-limiter sharding.” Bounded inaccuracy, low latency, no per-request central call.

Picker: edge of system or strict billing-quota? Centralized. High-throughput per-call cost-sensitive? Decentralized or hybrid. Always design for the rate-limiter being unavailable — fail-open or fail-closed is a deliberate choice, not a default.

Gotcha: If your rate limiter fails closed (blocks traffic on Redis outage), Redis becomes a single point of failure. If it fails open, an attacker can DDoS your rate limiter to bypass it. The production answer is fail-open with circuit breaking on the rate-limiter call itself, plus a stricter local fallback limit.
Tier-based / per-tenant / per-API-key shaping: how do you design the policy?

Policy = a hierarchy of buckets. Real-world structure:

  • Global: protect total fleet capacity. Hard cap that nobody can blow past.
  • Per-tenant: fairness across customers. Free tier vs Enterprise tier — different rates, different burst capacities.
  • Per-API-key: identifies which integration is misbehaving inside a tenant.
  • Per-endpoint: expensive endpoints (search, exports) get tighter limits than cheap ones (health, status).
  • Per-IP: anonymous abuse protection on public endpoints.

A request must pass every applicable bucket. Order matters for cost: cheap checks first, expensive (Redis) last.

Common production extension: cost-weighted limits (a search request consumes 10 tokens; a getById consumes 1) — lets you express “100 cheap or 10 expensive per minute” in one policy. AWS, GitHub GraphQL, Algolia all do this.

What's the right 429 response, and what does Retry-After mean?

HTTP 429 Too Many Requests (RFC 6585). The polite, machine-readable way to say no. The response should include:

  • Retry-After: either a delta-seconds integer or an HTTP-date. Tells the client when it’s reasonable to retry. RFC 7231 defines the syntax; most clients honor it.
  • X-RateLimit-Limit, X-RateLimit-Remaining, X-RateLimit-Reset (or the IETF draft RateLimit headers): tells the client its current state proactively, so it doesn’t need to bump up against the limit to find it.
  • A clear error body identifying the exhausted bucket ("limit": "tenant_per_minute") so debugging doesn’t require reading docs.

Don’t use 503 (server unavailable) for rate limiting — clients treat 503 as “system broken, alert humans,” and 429 as “back off, retry.” The semantic distinction matters for monitoring and client retry logic.

Retry-After: 12
RateLimit-Limit: 100, 100;w=60
RateLimit-Remaining: 0
RateLimit-Reset: 12
Content-Type: application/json

{ "error": "rate_limited", "bucket": "tenant_per_minute", "retry_after": 12 }
Exponential backoff with jitter: why jitter matters and what kind to use.

Plain exponential backoff (1s, 2s, 4s, 8s…) is correct in spirit but synchronizes failures: every client that hit the same downstream blip retries at the same offsets, creating retry waves. The fix is jitter — randomize within or around the backoff window so retries spread out.

Three flavors, named in the canonical AWS Architecture Blog post (Marc Brooker, 2015):

  • Full jitter: sleep = random(0, base * 2^n). Most spread, most randomness. Default recommendation for client retries.
  • Equal jitter: sleep = base * 2^n / 2 + random(0, base * 2^n / 2). Less random, but guarantees a minimum backoff so a client won’t retry instantly.
  • Decorrelated jitter: sleep = min(cap, random(base, prev * 3)). Each retry’s sleep depends on the previous one, not the iteration count. Recovers faster than exp from a single transient.

For competing-clients-on-shared-resource scenarios, full jitter is the proven win. For single-client-restoring-after-blip, decorrelated jitter is faster on average.

Gotcha: “Exponential backoff” without jitter is the canonical retry-storm contributor. If a candidate says “we use exponential backoff” without mentioning jitter, that’s a follow-up question.
Rate limiting vs throttling vs quotas vs concurrency limits: tease them apart.

People use these interchangeably; they’re distinct controls.

  • Rate limit: requests per unit time (100/min). Reject excess.
  • Throttle: rate limit that delays rather than rejects (or shapes traffic). The old name; modern usage often = rate-limit.
  • Quota: total budget over a long period (1M requests/month). Billing-tied. Reset on calendar boundaries, not on rolling windows.
  • Concurrency limit: max in-flight requests at once (50 simultaneous). Independent of rate — you can have low rate but high concurrency (slow long polls) or high rate but low concurrency (fast hits).

You almost always need both rate and concurrency: rate stops bursts, concurrency stops slow-runaway. A 100 rps limit doesn’t protect you from 1000 simultaneous slow requests holding connections.

AWS API Gateway exposes all four explicitly. Stripe enforces rate + concurrency separately. Get used to writing them as orthogonal axes.

How do you rate-limit a streaming or long-polling API?

Standard request-per-second rate limiting doesn’t fit. Three patterns instead:

  • Concurrency cap: N open streams per tenant. Connection-establish gets a token, drop on disconnect. Simple, robust.
  • Bandwidth shaping: bytes/sec per stream. Common in object storage downloads (S3 has tiered limits per bucket and per request).
  • Event-rate limiting on the stream: count emitted messages, not connection establishment. WebSockets, SSE, gRPC streaming.

The other piece: backpressure. If the consumer is slow, the protocol must signal it (gRPC flow control, HTTP/2 windows, application-level credit-based flow). Without backpressure, “rate-limiting” the producer is just delayed buffering.

Distributed rate limiter implementation in Redis: what's the right primitive?

Three production patterns, in order of sophistication:

  • Fixed-window with INCR: key = limit:{user}:{minute}; INCR + EXPIRE. Atomic, dumb, two ops. Boundary-burst weakness applies.
  • Sliding window with sorted set: key = limit:{user}; ZADD with timestamp, ZREMRANGEBYSCORE for expired entries, ZCARD for count. Accurate, but memory grows with rate; only feasible for moderate-rate keys.
  • Token bucket via Lua script: single atomic script reads {tokens, last_refill}, updates, returns allow/deny. The standard for production. Used by Stripe, AWS limiters, and the open-source Upstash ratelimit library.
-- Token-bucket Lua, atomic in Redis
local tokens = tonumber(redis.call("HGET", KEYS[1], "tokens") or ARGV[1])
local last   = tonumber(redis.call("HGET", KEYS[1], "last") or ARGV[3])
local now    = tonumber(ARGV[3])
tokens = math.min(tonumber(ARGV[1]), tokens + (now - last) * tonumber(ARGV[2]))
if tokens >= 1 then
  redis.call("HMSET", KEYS[1], "tokens", tokens-1, "last", now)
  return 1
end
return 0
What is “adaptive” or “dynamic” rate limiting?

Limits that adjust based on system health rather than fixed policy. Used at scale where static limits are either too generous (during a partial outage, you accept too much load) or too tight (during recovery, you needlessly reject).

Patterns:

  • Load-shedding by health: p99 latency > threshold → tighten limits proportionally. Netflix’s concurrency-limits library does this.
  • AIMD (Additive Increase, Multiplicative Decrease): the same control law TCP uses. Increase quota slowly when accept rate is good; halve on saturation.
  • Concurrency limit via TCP-Vegas-style detection: measure latency at increasing concurrency; back off when latency knee detected. Used in Netflix’s adaptive limiter and in Envoy’s adaptive concurrency filter.

The honest trade-off: adaptive limiters are harder to debug (“why was my request rejected?” → “system was sick at the time”) and harder to set SLOs against. Most teams keep static limits and add adaptive on top as an outer ring of defense.

How do you avoid “tarpit” behavior — rate-limiting expensive operations after the work is done?

The bad pattern: client sends a request, server runs it, then rejects with 429 because the rate-limiter wasn’t consulted until the end. The server pays the cost; the client gets nothing. Worst-of-both.

Right patterns:

  • Limit at admission: check before doing any expensive work. The standard.
  • Cost-weighted post-charge: deduct tokens proportional to actual cost after measuring (e.g., search query that scanned N rows costs f(N) tokens). Combined with a soft pre-admission check to avoid runaway. AWS DynamoDB on-demand mode does this with consumed capacity.
  • Reserve-and-confirm: reserve N tokens before, refund unused after. Lets you size the worst-case but only pay actual.

For very expensive operations (large exports, batch jobs), reject upfront with quotas, then do the work async with progress tracking — not synchronous rate-limiting against a single API call.

§ 03  Circuit breakers, retries, bulkheads

Failing fast, failing isolated, failing cheap.

Three primitives that, used together, turn a fragile service mesh into a robust one. Circuit breakers stop you from hammering a sick downstream. Retries let you absorb transient failure — if you back off correctly. Bulkheads stop one bad dependency from sinking the ship. The defensive triad of every modern microservice.

Concept · The three states of a breaker Closed: requests flow; failures are counted in a sliding window. Open: failures crossed a threshold; all requests fail-fast without hitting downstream. After a cool-off, the breaker enters half-open: it allows a probe request (or a small number) through. Success closes the breaker; failure re-opens it. The whole point: stop calling something that’s broken, give it time to recover, and don’t pile on retries during the outage.

Hystrix (Netflix, 2012) popularized the pattern; it’s now archived (since 2018, in maintenance mode forever). Modern stacks use Resilience4j (Java/Kotlin), Polly (.NET), opossum (Node), or proxy-level breakers in Envoy / Istio. If a candidate says “we use Hystrix,” ask when they last upgraded.
Walk through the closed → open → half-open state machine.

Closed (normal): calls pass through. The breaker tracks failures over a sliding window (count-based or time-based) and computes a failure rate. If the rate crosses a threshold (e.g., 50% over the last 100 calls), trip to open.

Open (tripped): all calls fail-fast immediately with a CallNotPermittedException (or whatever your library calls it). No load on the downstream. After a configured wait time (the “sleep window” — typically seconds to minutes), the breaker moves to half-open.

Half-open (probing): allow a small number of calls through (Resilience4j defaults to 10). If they succeed at the threshold rate, close the breaker. If they fail, re-open and start the sleep window again.

Tunables that matter: failure-rate threshold (50% is the usual default), minimum-calls before evaluation (don’t trip on 1-of-1), sliding window (count or time-based), wait duration in open. Resilience4j has all four; tune to your traffic shape.

// Resilience4j Java config
CircuitBreakerConfig cfg = CircuitBreakerConfig.custom()
    .failureRateThreshold(50)
    .minimumNumberOfCalls(20)
    .slidingWindowType(SlidingWindowType.COUNT_BASED)
    .slidingWindowSize(100)
    .waitDurationInOpenState(Duration.ofSeconds(30))
    .permittedNumberOfCallsInHalfOpenState(10)
    .build();
Why is Hystrix archived, and what should you use instead?

Netflix open-sourced Hystrix in 2012, archived it in 2018. Their reasoning: their internal needs evolved (they moved to adaptive concurrency limits), the community had matured equivalent libraries, and the bridge cost wasn’t worth maintaining.

Modern alternatives:

  • Resilience4j (Java/Kotlin) — built on Java 8 functional, modular (separate breaker / retry / bulkhead / rate-limiter modules), micrometer-friendly. The default for new JVM code.
  • Polly (.NET) — the de facto .NET resilience library; excellent ergonomics.
  • opossum (Node) — the standard Node circuit breaker.
  • Envoy / Istio — sidecar/proxy-level breakers. The right answer for polyglot fleets where you don’t want to maintain a breaker library per language.

The other shift: Netflix’s own current pattern is concurrency limits (the concurrency-limits library), which is more like adaptive admission control than a classic breaker. For most teams, Resilience4j is still the right starting point.

Why do retries amplify outages without backoff?

Suppose service A calls B; B is at capacity and starts timing out 50% of requests. A retries each timeout once. Effective load on B = 1.5x. B gets sicker; timeout rate climbs to 80%; retry rate climbs to 1.8x. B falls over.

Then A’s callers retry their failed calls to A; A’s effective input rate rises; even if B were to recover, A is now also overloaded. This is the classic retry storm — a small downstream blip cascades into a full-stack outage.

The defenses, layered:

  • Backoff with jitter (see §02). Spread retries in time so the recovery window isn’t crushed.
  • Retry budgets: at most X% of total requests can be retries. If failure rate is high, the budget caps total retry traffic.
  • Circuit breakers: open the breaker once failure rate is high, eliminate retries against an unhealthy downstream.
  • Bounded retry count: usually 1-2 max for synchronous calls; never unbounded.
  • Don’t retry on the wrong status: 4xx is usually a client error (don’t retry); 5xx and timeouts may be retriable; 429 retry only after Retry-After.
Probing: “You see CPU and error rate spike together on a downstream. Did your retries cause it?” The right answer says: maybe; let’s look at retry rate as a percentage of total. If >30%, retries are amplifying. The metric to watch: retries-per-original-request, not just absolute count.
What is a “retry budget” and how does it bound storm risk?

A retry budget caps retries as a fraction of original requests. Example policy: “at most 10% of recent requests can be retries.” If 100 original requests are flying, only 10 retries can launch in the same window; further retries are dropped (or bypass to fail-fast).

Implementation: token bucket where success and original requests refill the bucket; retries consume from it. If success rate is high, plenty of budget; if everything is failing, budget runs out and retries stop — the system stops attacking itself.

Used by Google’s gRPC retries (the gRPC retry policy explicitly supports budgets) and Envoy’s retry filter. The principle: retries should be cheap when things are healthy and forbidden when things are broken.

# Envoy retry policy with budget
retry_policy:
  retry_on: "5xx,connect-failure"
  num_retries: 2
  retry_budget:
    budget_percent: 20.0
    min_retry_concurrency: 3
What is a hedged request, and when is it appropriate?

A hedged request sends a duplicate request to a second backend after a delay (typically the p95 latency); whichever responds first wins, the other is canceled. Cuts tail latency without paying for full duplication.

Coined in Jeff Dean’s “The Tail at Scale” (CACM, 2013). The economics: if you hedge after p95, you only duplicate 5% of requests, but those 5% are the slow ones — the ones blowing your p99. A small extra load for a meaningful tail-latency reduction.

Conditions to use:

  • Idempotent operations only (or you risk double-execution). Reads are the natural fit.
  • Multiple backends available (replicas, multiple shards).
  • Cancel-on-first-response support (gRPC supports cancellation natively; HTTP/1 doesn’t cleanly).
  • Tail latency is the bottleneck, not throughput. If you’re saturated, hedging makes things worse.

BigTable, Spanner, and several CDN edge fetches all use hedged requests internally. gRPC has a built-in hedgingPolicy in service config.

What is an idempotency key, and why is it required for safe retries?

An idempotency key is a client-generated unique value (UUID, request hash) attached to a non-idempotent operation. The server records the key + result; on retry with the same key, returns the cached result instead of re-executing.

The motivating problem: a POST to /charge is non-idempotent — retrying double-charges. With an idempotency key, the server says “I’ve seen key X, here’s the original result” on retry. Stripe popularized the pattern (Idempotency-Key header); GitHub, Square, Adyen, every modern payments API supports it.

Implementation:

  • Server stores (key, request_hash, response) with TTL (usually 24h).
  • Same key + same request → return cached response (the canonical case).
  • Same key + different request → return error (the request mutated under retry).
  • Storage: usually a database row keyed by tenant + idempotency key.

Without an idempotency key, your only safe retry is the GET. Everything else risks a double-write. This is why “safe to retry” is a property of both the operation and the protocol.

Gotcha: “PUT is idempotent” is technically true at the HTTP-semantic level but doesn’t help if your handler isn’t. If a PUT triggers side effects (email, billing, third-party call), it’s functionally non-idempotent. Idempotency is an end-to-end property, not just an HTTP method.
Bulkheads: thread-pool isolation vs semaphore isolation, and when which?

The metaphor: a ship’s hull divided into watertight compartments so one breach doesn’t sink the ship. Translated: each downstream dependency gets its own concurrency budget; one slow dependency can’t starve the threads serving others.

  • Thread-pool isolation: a dedicated thread pool per dependency. Calls are submitted to the pool; if the pool is full, the call rejects. Stops a hung dependency from holding caller threads. Cost: thread overhead, context switching, no JIT-friendly call site (calls cross threads).
  • Semaphore isolation: a counter limiting concurrent in-flight calls per dependency. Caller thread continues to run; if semaphore is exhausted, reject. Lower overhead. Cost: doesn’t protect against blocking calls (the caller thread is still stuck waiting).

Picker:

  • Synchronous, possibly-blocking call (legacy JDBC, blocking HTTP client) → thread pool — you need to be able to time out and abandon.
  • Asynchronous, non-blocking call (NIO HTTP, reactive client) → semaphore — you control concurrency without paying threading overhead.
  • Virtual threads (Java 21+) shift the trade-off — semaphore-style limits over virtual threads is the modern pattern.

Resilience4j has both: ThreadPoolBulkhead and SemaphoreBulkhead. Hystrix favored thread pools by default; modern libs default to semaphores.

Why are timeouts the most underrated resilience primitive?

Without a timeout, a hung downstream consumes a caller thread (or coroutine) forever. The system doesn’t fail; it slowly fills with stuck threads, runs out of capacity, and eventually no requests can be accepted. The classic “thread pool exhaustion” cascade.

Rules:

  • Every network call has a timeout. No exceptions. Default settings often have none (e.g., older Java HTTP clients with no read timeout, JDBC with infinite query timeout).
  • Timeout shorter than your caller’s timeout. If your caller times out at 5s and you wait 30s for downstream, you’re wasting the next 25s on work nobody wants. Cascading timeouts — each layer slightly shorter.
  • Connect timeout vs read timeout vs total deadline. Connect = TCP handshake; read = waiting for bytes; deadline = whole operation. All three matter; pick conservatively.

The first thing you should ask in a resilience review: “Show me the timeout values on every external call.” If anyone has to look it up, that’s your finding.

Probing: “Why don’t we just retry forever instead of timing out?” Because the caller above you isn’t waiting. Timeouts let you free up capacity for new work; without them, your backlog grows unboundedly.
What is deadline propagation, and why does gRPC bake it in?

A deadline is an absolute timestamp: “respond by 12:00:01.500”. Propagated through every downstream call, so each layer knows the time it has left and shortens its own timeout to fit.

The motivating problem: client says “I’ll wait 1 second.” Service A spends 600ms, then calls B with default 1-second timeout. B might respond at 1500ms, but client gave up at 1000ms — 500ms of B’s work is wasted. With deadline propagation, A passes “you have 400ms” to B; B aborts at 400ms or hands back early.

gRPC propagates deadlines in metadata automatically; the server checks context.Deadline() and aborts work past it. HTTP doesn’t have first-class deadline support; you fake it with a X-Deadline-Ms header and a middleware that wires it into your timeout context.

This is one of the under-appreciated reasons gRPC is operationally nicer than REST at scale. If you’re running REST, write a deadline-propagation middleware on day one; you’ll thank yourself.

How do circuit breakers, retries, bulkheads, and timeouts compose? In what order?

The composition matters. The standard outer-to-inner order:

Bulkhead (admission)
   → Rate limit (admission)
      → Circuit breaker (don't even try if downstream is broken)
         → Retry (with backoff & budget)
            → Timeout (per attempt)
               → Actual call

Why this order:

  • Bulkhead and rate limit are admission — reject before doing any work.
  • Circuit breaker is before retries — you don’t retry into an open breaker.
  • Timeout is per attempt — each retry attempt has its own timeout, total is bounded by retry policy.

Resilience4j’s decorator chain follows this order in its DSL. Polly likewise. The wrong order (e.g., retry outside breaker) defeats the breaker’s purpose — you’d retry through an open breaker and not save the downstream from your traffic.

What's the difference between a circuit breaker and a fallback?

Circuit breaker = the decision not to call a downstream when it’s sick. Fallback = what you do instead: return a default, hit a cache, return a degraded response, or fail with a graceful error.

They’re orthogonal but usually paired. Open breaker without fallback = fail-fast 5xx (better than waiting, but still a failure). Open breaker with cache fallback = serve stale data (might be acceptable). Open breaker with degraded-response fallback (“recommendations unavailable, here’s a curated default”) = the user might not even notice.

The fallback question is mostly a product question: what’s the right behavior when this dependency is gone? Sometimes a real cache; sometimes a sentinel; sometimes a graceful empty result. Sometimes the answer is “there is no acceptable fallback, fail loud” — payment confirmations, audit logs, etc.

What is “graceful degradation” and how do you architect for it?

Graceful degradation = the system continues to provide core value when peripheral dependencies fail. The Amazon detail page that still loads when the recommendations service is down, the dashboard that shows main charts when one widget’s data is missing.

Architecturally, three patterns:

  • Critical path vs nice-to-have: classify every call. Critical = block the response; nice-to-have = render in parallel, give up after timeout, render without it.
  • Fan-out with bulkheads: many parallel calls in a page-load; each in its own bulkhead. Slow ones drop their content but don’t hold the page.
  • Cache as a fallback layer: stale-while-error patterns — serve the last-known-good when origin fails.

The cultural piece: product needs to define the degraded state. Engineers can’t unilaterally decide “let’s just hide the recommendations carousel.” This is the kind of question a staff-level loop tests — can you have the conversation with PMs about acceptable fallbacks?

§ 04  Outbox & integration patterns

Atomic publish without the two-phase commit.

Every microservices system eventually hits the dual-write problem: write to the database and publish a message to a broker, atomically. Doing this naively gives you lost events, duplicate events, or worse. The transactional outbox is the modern answer — a small pattern with massive correctness implications.

Concept · The dual-write problem You want to update an order row and emit an OrderPlaced event. If you do them in two steps, four bad outcomes are possible: (1) DB ok, broker fails → lost event; (2) broker ok, DB fails → phantom event; (3) both ok but client retried → duplicate event; (4) DB ok, process crashes before broker call → lost event.

The transactional outbox writes the event into an outbox table in the same database transaction as the business write. A separate process reads the outbox and publishes. Atomicity is borrowed from the DB transaction; publish is best-effort with retries. Correct, simple, no two-phase commit. The default pattern at every modern event-driven shop.
Walk through the transactional outbox pattern step by step.

Schema: an outbox table with at minimum (id, aggregate_type, aggregate_id, event_type, payload, created_at, published_at). Optional: partition key, headers, ordering metadata.

Producer side:

  1. Begin transaction.
  2. Insert/update the business row(s).
  3. Insert the outbox row(s) describing what happened.
  4. Commit. Now both are durable atomically.

Relay (publisher):

  1. Read unpublished outbox rows (polling or CDC).
  2. Publish to broker (Kafka, RabbitMQ, SNS, etc.).
  3. Mark as published (or delete the row) on broker ack.
  4. On failure, leave for retry; idempotent because consumers dedupe (see inbox).
-- Pseudo-DDL
CREATE TABLE outbox (
  id           UUID      PRIMARY KEY,
  aggregate    TEXT      NOT NULL,
  event_type   TEXT      NOT NULL,
  payload      JSONB     NOT NULL,
  created_at   TIMESTAMPTZ DEFAULT now(),
  published_at TIMESTAMPTZ
);
CREATE INDEX ON outbox (created_at) WHERE published_at IS NULL;

The brilliance: you traded “atomic across DB and broker” for “atomic within DB” (free) plus “eventually published with at-least-once” (cheap, with idempotent consumers).

Polling outbox vs CDC-based outbox: when which?

Polling: a worker repeatedly queries SELECT ... FROM outbox WHERE published_at IS NULL ORDER BY created_at LIMIT N FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED, publishes, marks published. Simple, language-agnostic, works with any DB.

Cost: latency floor = polling interval (typically 100ms–1s); load on the DB scales with worker count; ordering across workers is hard if you parallelize.

CDC-based: a tool reads the database’s replication log (Postgres WAL, MySQL binlog, MongoDB oplog) and streams changes. Debezium is the dominant open-source CDC; AWS DMS, Striim, and Maxwell are alternatives.

Cost: more moving parts (Debezium → Kafka Connect → topics), schema-registry overhead, requires DB log access (not always granted on managed DBs). Win: sub-100ms end-to-end latency, no polling load, ordering preserved per primary key.

Picker:

  • Low volume, simple ops, fits-in-one-deploy: polling. Don’t over-engineer.
  • High throughput (>1k events/s), latency-sensitive, multi-service consumers: CDC.
  • Multi-DC, want strong ordering with sharded consumers: CDC.
Probing: “Why not just publish directly from the application after commit?” The honest answer: process can crash between commit and publish. Outbox makes the publish recoverable from durable state.
Why does FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED matter for polling outbox workers?

If you run multiple polling workers (for throughput or HA), they’ll race on the same unpublished rows. Without locking, you publish duplicates. With FOR UPDATE alone, workers wait on each other — serial throughput.

FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED (Postgres 9.5+, also MySQL 8.0+) lets each worker grab an unlocked batch and skip rows other workers are processing. Linear scaling with worker count, no duplicates, no waiting.

SELECT id, payload FROM outbox
 WHERE published_at IS NULL
 ORDER BY created_at
 LIMIT 100
 FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED;

The pattern generalizes: any “workers consume from a queue table” design wants this. SKIP LOCKED is one of those features that, once you know it, you wonder how you survived without.

What is the inbox pattern, and why do you need it?

If outbox makes producers correct, inbox makes consumers correct. The producer publishes at-least-once; consumers must dedupe. Inbox is the consumer-side dedup table.

Schema: a small table keyed on (message_id), with the result of processing. On consume:

  1. Begin transaction.
  2. Try to insert (message_id, processed_at, ...). If UNIQUE violation, this is a duplicate — commit empty.
  3. Otherwise, do the business logic + insert.
  4. Commit.

The unique-constraint-on-message-id is the dedup primitive. Combined with idempotent business logic where possible, this gives you exactly-once effects (not exactly-once delivery — that’s impossible in general; see Lindsay/Kreps).

Inbox is mandatory if your message-id is unique. If your message-id can repeat (rebroadcast on rebalance), use a deterministic hash of payload + sequence number as the dedup key.

Saga vs outbox: they solve different problems — which?

People sometimes conflate them. They solve different problems and often coexist.

Outbox: “how do I atomically publish an event when I commit a DB write?” A communication / publishing pattern. Lives at the boundary of one service.

Saga: “how do I orchestrate a multi-step business transaction across services without 2PC?” A long-running workflow pattern. Each step is a local transaction; failures trigger compensating transactions to undo earlier steps.

Two saga flavors:

  • Choreography: services emit events; other services react. No central orchestrator. Decoupled but hard to trace.
  • Orchestration: a central orchestrator (Camunda, Temporal, Step Functions) drives the saga. Visible workflow, easier to debug. Modern default.

The interplay: each saga step uses outbox to atomically commit its local change and emit the event the next step listens for. Sagas orchestrate; outbox publishes. One handles the multi-step coordination; the other handles atomic event emission.

Why is two-phase commit (2PC) the default-no answer in modern microservices?

2PC (XA transactions): a coordinator asks all participants to prepare; once all ack, asks them to commit. Any participant can abort, all must roll back. Atomicity guaranteed across heterogeneous systems — if it works.

Why people stopped:

  • Blocking on coordinator failure: if the coordinator dies after “prepare”, all participants hold locks waiting forever (or until manual intervention). The pathological “in-doubt” state.
  • Latency cost: two round-trips to every participant; locks held throughout. Crushes throughput at scale.
  • Operational fragility: XA-aware drivers, distributed-transaction managers, recovery logs — a fragile stack rarely battle-tested in 2026.
  • Doesn’t cross arbitrary systems: Kafka famously doesn’t do XA; many cloud services don’t.

The modern answer: outbox + saga + idempotency. Sacrifices distributed atomicity for local atomicity + eventual consistency + correctness via idempotent operations. Cheaper, more available, less fragile. 2PC isn’t wrong — just rarely the right call outside legacy enterprise integrations.

Gotcha: “We use distributed transactions” on a microservices architecture is a red flag in modern interviews. The follow-up: “What happens when the coordinator fails between prepare and commit?” Most candidates can’t answer; that’s the point of the question.
What is the “lost commit” failure mode?

Without outbox, the dangerous interleaving: you write to the DB, commit, then the process crashes (or network partitions to broker) before publishing. The DB has the change; no event was emitted; downstream services are forever out of sync.

Even with retries, the problem is that nobody knows the publish needed to happen after the process crashes. The intent died with the process. Outbox solves this: the intent is durable in the DB; on restart, polling/CDC will pick it up.

The other lost-commit variant: you publish to the broker first, broker acks, then DB write fails. Now the event is out, downstream acted on it, but your local state contradicts. This is why the order matters: DB first, broker second, both inside the outbox transaction.

How do you preserve event ordering with outbox + Kafka?

Kafka guarantees per-partition ordering. To preserve ordering for an aggregate, all its events must land in the same partition — meaning you partition by aggregate ID (e.g., order ID).

Pieces:

  • Outbox row carries a partition key (usually the aggregate ID).
  • Relay publishes with that key; Kafka routes to the same partition.
  • Relay must publish in created_at order for that key — with multiple workers, partition the work by hash of aggregate ID so one worker owns one aggregate’s events.
  • CDC handles this for you — Debezium reads the WAL in commit order and emits events with consistent partitioning.

The trickier case is global ordering, which Kafka can’t do across partitions. If you need global order, you’re looking at a single partition (won’t scale) or a single ordered queue with a different broker. Most domain events don’t need global order; per-aggregate is plenty.

What's the difference between at-most-once, at-least-once, and exactly-once?
  • At-most-once: messages may be lost, never duplicated. Achieved by: don’t retry. Used in metrics ingestion, telemetry, where loss is preferable to duplication.
  • At-least-once: messages may be duplicated, never lost. Achieved by: producer retries on failure, consumer acks after processing. The default for most event systems. Requires idempotent consumers to be correct.
  • Exactly-once: each message effectively processed once. Generally impossible across heterogeneous systems — what you actually achieve is at-least-once delivery + idempotent processing = exactly-once effect.

Kafka’s “exactly-once semantics” (transactions across producer + consumer + state store) work within Kafka but break the moment you write to anything else. The honest framing: aim for at-least-once delivery + idempotent everything — that’s the exactly-once you can actually deliver.

Probing: “Can you give me exactly-once delivery?” The right answer is: not across heterogeneous systems — here’s how I get exactly-once effects instead. The wrong answer is: yes, Kafka has it.
How do you handle outbox table growth in production?

Outbox is high-write (every business write adds a row) and high-read for the relay. Without retention discipline, it’ll be the biggest table in your DB by month two.

Patterns:

  • Delete-on-publish: the relay deletes the row instead of marking it. Fast cleanup; outbox stays small. Risk: lost rows are gone, no audit trail. Common.
  • Soft-delete + scheduled cleanup: mark published_at, run a daily job that deletes rows older than N days. Audit trail for a window. Standard in regulated industries.
  • Partitioned outbox: Postgres declarative partitioning by day; drop old partitions. O(1) cleanup, no DELETE bloat.
  • Move to archive table: for compliance, copy old outbox rows to a cold-storage table or S3.

Whatever you choose, monitor outbox size and “oldest unpublished” age. Both are key SLOs of the publishing pipeline. A growing oldest-unpublished is the leading indicator that the relay is broken.

What about the “listen / notify” pattern as an outbox alternative?

Postgres LISTEN/NOTIFY: an in-DB pub/sub channel. The producer writes a row + sends a NOTIFY in the same transaction; consumers LISTEN on the channel and react.

Tempting but flawed for outbox use:

  • NOTIFY is in-memory — if the listener disconnects, it misses notifications. There’s no replay mechanism.
  • Listeners must be live at the moment of NOTIFY. After a deploy, you’ll silently miss events.
  • Doesn’t scale across instances cleanly — every listener gets every notification.

It’s a useful complement to outbox: write to outbox table + NOTIFY; relay listens for the wake-up to avoid polling latency. If the listener misses a NOTIFY, polling catches it on the next interval. Don’t use NOTIFY instead of the durable outbox table.

§ 05  Caching strategies

Two hard problems, one of them is invalidation.

Caching is the easiest 10x speedup in your system — until it isn’t, and you ship a stale price to a customer or melt the origin during a stampede. The interesting interview material is not “what is a cache” but the failure modes: cold-cache thundering herds, hot keys, dogpiles, stale-after-write. Get these right and your cache is invisible; get them wrong and it’s the loudest pager source you have.

Concept · Five canonical patterns Cache-aside (lazy load): app reads cache, misses, fetches from origin, populates cache. The default; works with any cache & store. Read-through: cache library fetches origin on miss; transparent to app. Write-through: writes go through cache to origin synchronously; cache is always fresh. Write-behind (write-back): writes hit cache immediately, async-flushed to origin; faster writes, durability risk. Refresh-ahead: cache proactively refreshes hot keys before expiry, avoiding miss latency.

Cache-aside is the workhorse of the industry. The other patterns are right when you have a cache library that does origin I/O for you (Caffeine’s LoadingCache, Spring Cache abstractions, Hazelcast). Pick deliberately; don’t mix without thinking.
Cache-aside vs read-through: when does the distinction matter?

Cache-aside: the application code knows about the cache. Read code: val = cache.get(k); if val == null: val = db.get(k); cache.set(k, val). Write code: write to DB, invalidate cache (or just let TTL expire it). Most flexible; tied tightly to your choice of cache & store.

Read-through: the cache library knows how to load on miss. Application code: val = cache.get(k); the library calls a registered loader function on miss, populates, returns. Caffeine’s LoadingCache<K,V>, Guava’s CacheLoader, Hazelcast’s MapLoader.

The advantage of read-through: built-in stampede protection. A good loading cache uses internal lock-on-miss so only one thread loads any given key; concurrent callers wait. With cache-aside, you implement that yourself or you’ll dogpile.

Picker: in-process cache → read-through (Caffeine). Distributed cache (Redis, Memcached) → cache-aside is more common because the loader pattern is awkward across processes.

Write-through vs write-behind: durability vs latency.

Write-through: write hits cache + origin synchronously. Cache is always consistent with origin; on cache loss, no data loss. Cost: every write pays cache + DB latency.

Write-behind: write hits cache; cache buffers and flushes to origin asynchronously. Faster writes (cache-only latency); higher throughput (batched flushes). Cost: data loss window if cache crashes before flush; consistency window between cache and origin.

Picker:

  • Write-through: financial, identity, anything where loss is unacceptable. Modest latency penalty, full safety.
  • Write-behind: counters, rate-limit tallies, view counts, telemetry — cases where occasional loss is fine and writes are heavy. Hazelcast’s write-behind, Redis with periodic AOF flush.

The classic disaster: someone configures Redis as a primary store with write-behind, the node fails, hours of writes evaporate. Don’t use write-behind for primary persistence unless you’ve thought hard about the durability story.

Compare LRU, LFU, ARC, 2Q, and W-TinyLFU. Which does Caffeine use, and why?
  • LRU (Least Recently Used): evict the entry untouched longest. Cheap, ubiquitous, but bad on scan-heavy workloads (a single sweep evicts your hot set).
  • LFU (Least Frequently Used): evict the least-accessed. Resists scans but slow to adapt to changing hot sets; old-popular items linger.
  • ARC (Adaptive Replacement Cache): IBM’s patent (now expired) blending recency + frequency via two LRU lists with a ghost-list tuner. Hard to implement; patent-encumbered for years.
  • 2Q: split into a small FIFO “probationary” queue and an LRU “hot” queue. Items enter probationary; touched again, promoted to hot. Better than LRU on scans.
  • W-TinyLFU: a small LRU window in front of a large LFU main, with a frequency sketch (Count-Min Sketch) approximating frequency cheaply. Near-optimal hit rates on most workloads.

Caffeine (the modern Java cache library, by Ben Manes) uses W-TinyLFU as default. The published benchmarks vs Guava’s LRU and LinkedHashMap variants consistently show 5–15% better hit rate at the same size, with similar throughput.

The takeaway for an interview: knowing why the in-process cache landscape moved from LRU to W-TinyLFU is the staff-level signal. “We use LRU” is fine for a basic cache; it’s not the optimal modern answer.

What is a cache stampede / thundering herd, and how do you prevent it?

Stampede: a popular key expires; many concurrent requests miss simultaneously; all hit origin; origin gets overloaded. The classic Black Friday cache failure pattern.

Defenses, in order of sophistication:

  • Lock-on-miss: first miss acquires a (Redis/local) lock; other concurrent misses wait or return stale. Simple, effective. Caffeine’s LoadingCache does this in-process.
  • Probabilistic early refresh (XFetch): as TTL approaches, each request has a small probability of triggering a background refresh. The probability rises near expiry. Distributes the refresh work across many requests instead of one stampede. Vattani et al., 2015.
  • Refresh-ahead: background job refreshes hot keys before they expire. Eliminates miss latency entirely for known-hot keys. Used by CDNs and high-traffic web apps.
  • Request coalescing: at the proxy / cache layer, identical concurrent requests for the same key merge into one origin call. NGINX has proxy_cache_lock; Varnish has request coalescing built-in.
  • TTL jitter: add random offset to TTLs so popular keys don’t expire simultaneously. TTL = base + random(0, base * 0.1). Cheapest defense; do this by default.
// XFetch: probability of early refresh rises as TTL approaches
if (now() > expiry - delta * beta * log(random())) {
  refreshAsync(key);
}
return cached;
What is a hot key, and how do you survive one?

A hot key is a single cache entry receiving disproportionate traffic — the celebrity user, the trending post, the “what’s the time” key everyone hits. In a sharded cache (Redis Cluster, Memcached consistent-hash), all that traffic lands on one node. The node becomes the bottleneck while others sit idle.

Mitigations:

  • Local L1 cache in front of the distributed cache: every app instance keeps a small in-process cache (Caffeine) for very hot keys. The hot key gets answered locally; only first-load and refresh hit the shared cache. The biggest single win.
  • Read replicas: Redis read-replicas, ProxySQL replica routing for cached reads.
  • Key splitting: shard a single hot value into N keys (celebrity:1#shard:0..N-1); writes update one of them; reads pick a random shard and merge. Practical for counters and aggregates.
  • Tiered TTL: longer TTL on hot data (refreshes are slower; load on origin is lower).

Detection: every cache should expose per-key (or per-key-pattern) hit/miss counters. Hot-key detection is its own design problem; Twitter’s “heavy hitters” sketch (top-K via Count-Min Sketch) is the standard.

Walk through the cache hierarchy: client → CDN → edge → origin cache → DB.

Each layer absorbs traffic from the next; only misses propagate down.

  • Client cache (browser, mobile app): HTTP Cache-Control, ETag/If-None-Match, Service Worker. Free; lowest latency. Bound by client trust (no secrets, watch private vs public).
  • CDN (Cloudflare, Akamai, Fastly, CloudFront): geographic edge; minutes-to-hours TTL on static and many dynamic assets. Highest cache hit rate by far.
  • Edge cache / API gateway cache (CloudFront edge, AWS API Gateway cache, Varnish in your stack): 1–60s TTL for hot dynamic responses.
  • Application cache (Caffeine in-process; Redis/Memcached distributed): seconds-to-minutes TTL; the workhorse layer for app data.
  • Database cache (Postgres shared_buffers, MySQL InnoDB buffer pool): bytes-to-blocks; the layer you don’t directly control but tune.

Design principle: cache as close to the consumer as the data’s freshness requirement allows. Logged-in user’s account page — can’t cache at CDN; can cache at app. Public marketing page — cache at CDN, edge, everywhere. The freshness budget shapes the cache placement.

What are negative caching and sentinel values, and when do you need them?

Negative caching: caching the result “this key doesn’t exist.” Without it, requests for missing keys re-hit origin every time — an attacker can DoS origin by requesting random keys (cache penetration).

Sentinel value: a special marker stored under the key, distinguishable from a real value, indicating “known not to exist.” Often a magic null marker or a special tombstone object.

Implementation:

  • On origin miss, store sentinel with short TTL (e.g., 30s — shorter than positive TTL because absent rows often appear).
  • On read, distinguish sentinel from real miss; return null/404 fast without origin call.

Defenses against cache-penetration attacks: negative caching + a Bloom filter at origin (“does this ID exist at all?”) + per-IP rate limiting on the public endpoint. Together they reduce origin load on missing-key floods to near zero.

Gotcha: if your “not found” representation is the same as your “cache miss” (both are null), you can’t distinguish them. Use an explicit sentinel; never let null mean two things in cache APIs.
Cache invalidation strategies: TTL-only, explicit purge, write-through, versioned keys.

Phil Karlton’s law: there are only two hard problems in computer science: cache invalidation and naming things. The four common approaches:

  • TTL-only: cache expires after N seconds; you tolerate up to N seconds of staleness. Simple, no coupling between writers and cache. Default for content where modest staleness is fine.
  • Explicit purge / invalidate-on-write: the writer (or its outbox-published event) sends a cache-delete message. Tight; depends on the invalidator never failing silently.
  • Write-through: every write goes through the cache, so the cache is always fresh. Tight, requires you write through the cache surface, not direct to DB.
  • Versioned keys: include a version (e.g., user:42:v17) in the key; bump version on write. Old versions become unreachable; TTL or LRU evicts. The most robust pattern for correctness; cost is extra read-the-version-first or version-included-in-data.

The honest combination most production systems use: TTL as a baseline + explicit invalidation for cases that matter. Versioned keys for high-correctness paths (entity bound to billing, security). Pure write-through is rare in distributed-cache setups.

What's wrong with “just delete the key on write” under concurrent access?

The race: a read misses, fetches from DB (returning value V1); meanwhile, a write commits V2 and deletes the cache. The read then writes V1 (the stale value) into the cache. Now the cache has V1 forever (until next TTL or write).

Fixes:

  • Read-then-version-check: include a version/timestamp in the cached value; on cache write, only set if no newer version exists (Redis WATCH/MULTI or SETNX patterns).
  • Delete-after-write with delay: first delete cache, then write DB, then delete again after a short delay (some Chinese microservices guides call this “cache-aside double delete”). The second delete catches stragglers from racing reads.
  • Don’t cache reads on misses; rely on write-through: removes the racing-read class entirely.
  • Versioned keys (above): a stale write to the wrong version is unreachable, so it doesn’t poison.

This race is the most-discussed cache correctness issue in production. If a candidate doesn’t mention it when asked “how would you invalidate after a write?”, push them.

How do you handle a cold cache after a deploy or cache-cluster restart?

Cache restart = empty cache = every request hits origin = origin overload. Common patterns:

  • Warm-up scripts: on deploy, run a job that pre-loads the top-N hottest keys (from prior cache stats or a known “essentials” list). Bring up the cache full of the things that matter.
  • Gradual traffic shift: after warm-up, ramp traffic 1% → 10% → 100% to the new cache. Lets origin breathe.
  • Multi-cluster cache with rolling deploy: never have all caches cold simultaneously. Critical at scale.
  • Rate limit at app layer on cache miss specifically: limit concurrent origin loads per node when miss rate spikes.
  • Stale-while-revalidate at HTTP layer: serve stale on miss while async refreshing. The browser/CDN does this for you with the right Cache-Control directives.

The lesson: a cache cluster is part of your capacity model. Don’t plan for 99% hit rate and have origin scaled for 1% miss; plan for 50% miss during a cache outage and ensure origin survives.

Distributed cache consistency: replication trade-offs in Redis Cluster.

Redis Cluster shards by hash slots (16,384 of them) across primaries; each primary has 0–N replicas. Writes go to the primary; replicas receive async replication (the default). Reads usually go to primaries (strong) or can be routed to replicas (eventual).

Trade-offs:

  • Async replication = lost writes on primary failover: WAIT command exists for synchronous-ish replication, but it’s rarely used in production due to latency cost.
  • Read-from-replica = stale reads: the lag is usually milliseconds, but occasional spikes happen. Acceptable for cache; not for “has this user accepted the new TOS”.
  • Cluster split-brain: a partition can elect a new primary; on heal, the old primary discards its data. With min-replicas-to-write, you can require N replicas alive to accept writes (poor man’s quorum).

For pure caching workloads, Redis’ eventual-consistency model is fine — the cache is best-effort by definition. For session storage, idempotency keys, distributed locks — the consistency story matters and you should think hard about whether Redis is right (or use Redlock with fencing tokens, or use a stronger store).

Probing: “Is Redis an appropriate store for distributed locks?” The Kleppmann-vs-antirez debate. Right answer: yes if you have fencing tokens and accept the limitations; no if you need strong correctness without external help. ZooKeeper / etcd are the conservative choice.
What is “serve-stale-on-error” and how do you implement it?

When origin fails, return the last cached value even if expired, rather than failing the user. A graceful degradation pattern that turns a backend outage into a slightly-stale user experience.

HTTP has standardized headers for this:

  • Cache-Control: stale-while-revalidate=60 — serve stale up to 60s past expiry while async-refreshing.
  • Cache-Control: stale-if-error=86400 — serve stale up to 24h on origin error.

CDN support: Cloudflare, Fastly, CloudFront all support these directives. NGINX supports proxy_cache_use_stale. RFC 5861 defines the semantics.

For app-level caches: write your loader to keep the previous value on load failure. Caffeine’s refreshAfterWrite doesn’t evict on refresh failure — you keep serving the old value until next refresh succeeds.

The subtle question: how stale is too stale? 5s for a price; hours for a static page. The user-acceptable staleness window is a product decision; the technical floor is “not so stale it’s wrong.”

§ 06  Observability & SRE

If you can't see it, it's not running.

Observability is the discipline of being able to ask new questions about a running system without redeploying. SRE is the discipline of running services with explicit reliability targets and engineering effort proportional to the gap. Together they form the operational spine of every modern SaaS — and the part of the interview most likely to expose whether you’ve actually run production.

Concept · Three pillars + correlation Metrics (numerical time-series, low-cardinality, cheap, aggregate-friendly — Prometheus, Datadog metrics, CloudWatch). Logs (high-cardinality structured records, verbose, expensive at scale — ELK, Loki, Splunk). Traces (causal request paths across services — Jaeger, Tempo, Datadog APM). The three are independent windows on the same reality; correlation via trace ID propagated through metrics tags and log fields lets you pivot from a metric anomaly to the exact failing request.

OpenTelemetry (CNCF, the merger of OpenCensus + OpenTracing) is the vendor-neutral instrumentation standard for all three (and now profiles, the “fourth pillar”). The shape of modern stacks: OTel SDK in your app → OTel Collector → vendor-specific backend(s). Lock-in moves from instrumentation to backend choice, which is the right axis to lock in on.
What is OpenTelemetry, and why is it the right default in 2026?

OpenTelemetry (OTel) is a CNCF project providing vendor-neutral APIs, SDKs, and protocols for telemetry. It covers traces, metrics, logs, and profiles — the four pillars now — with a single collection pipeline (OTLP, the OpenTelemetry Protocol).

The architecture:

  • SDK / auto-instrumentation: drops into your app; emits OTLP signals.
  • Collector: a sidecar/daemon process that receives OTLP, transforms (sampling, filtering, batching), and exports to one or more backends (Datadog, Honeycomb, Jaeger, Prometheus, Splunk, etc.).
  • Backend: your vendor of choice for storage and query.

Why default in 2026:

  • Vendor-neutral: change vendors without re-instrumenting. Massive long-term win.
  • Polyglot: SDKs for every major language; consistent semantic conventions across them.
  • Industry consensus: AWS, GCP, Azure, Datadog, Honeycomb, Splunk, every observability vendor accepts OTLP. The lock-in arms race ended.
Probing: “Why not just use Datadog/Honeycomb/… SDKs?” The honest answer: vendor SDKs are still fine for stable single-vendor shops. OTel is the correct answer when you might switch, run hybrid, or want a unified instrumentation story across many backends.
Counter, gauge, histogram, summary: when to use which?

The four canonical metric types (Prometheus, but the categories are universal):

  • Counter: monotonically increasing. http_requests_total, errors_total. Take the rate (rate(), increase()) to get RPS. Resets on process restart — rate functions handle that.
  • Gauge: can go up or down. Current values: memory_usage_bytes, active_connections, queue_depth. Take the latest, average, max.
  • Histogram: observations bucketed by value. http_request_duration_seconds. Pre-defined buckets at ingest; quantiles computed at query time. Aggregatable across instances.
  • Summary: like histogram, but quantiles computed client-side at observation time. Cheaper to query but not aggregatable across instances (you can’t average pre-computed p99s and get a meaningful global p99).

Modern advice: prefer histograms over summaries. Aggregation across instances/regions is the whole point of cloud-scale metrics; summaries fight you on this.

For latency specifically: histogram with sensible buckets (e.g., powers-of-2 or service-tuned). The classic mistake: histograms with too few buckets — you can’t reconstruct p99 if all your traffic falls in one bucket.

What is metric cardinality, and why is it the “high-cardinality tag” footgun?

Cardinality = unique combinations of metric name + label values. A counter http_requests_total{status="200", method="GET"} has low cardinality (~10 statuses × ~5 methods = 50 series). The same counter with user_id as a label and 1M users has 50M series. Cost — storage, ingest, query latency — scales linearly.

The trap: someone adds a label like request_id or user_id “for debugging”, the cardinality explodes, the metrics backend chokes, ingest costs spike, queries time out. Datadog, Prometheus, and most TSDBs have hard cardinality limits (Prometheus by memory; Datadog by billing tier).

Rules:

  • Labels must have bounded cardinality. Status codes yes, user IDs no.
  • If you need user-level breakdown, log it (logs are high-cardinality by design).
  • For traces: trace IDs are high-cardinality; that’s the right place. Don’t put trace IDs as metric labels.
  • Audit cardinality regularly — topk(100, count by (__name__) ({__name__=~".*"})) in Prometheus.
Gotcha: “We’ll just tag everything in metrics for flexibility” is the staff-level red flag. The candidate doesn’t understand the operational economics of TSDBs. Metrics are aggregates; logs are records; traces are causal paths. Put the data where its cardinality belongs.
Tracing: spans, baggage, sampling. What is head vs tail sampling?

A span = one unit of work (one DB call, one HTTP request) with start time, duration, attributes, and a parent span ID. Spans form a tree per request. The whole tree is a trace, identified by a traceId propagated via headers (traceparent in W3C Trace Context, RFC 9116-adjacent).

Baggage: key-value pairs attached to a trace and propagated with it. Used for cross-cutting context (tenant ID, feature-flag values). Don’t put PII or secrets here — baggage is sent in headers in cleartext between services.

Sampling — you don’t store every trace, you’d melt your storage:

  • Head sampling: decision made at the start of the trace (or at root). Probabilistic (e.g., 1% of requests) or deterministic (always trace if header set). Cheap, but you might drop the failed trace you needed.
  • Tail sampling: hold all spans of a trace in memory at the collector; once the trace completes, decide based on complete trace properties (was there an error? was latency > threshold?) whether to keep. Catches the interesting traces.
  • Adaptive sampling: dynamically adjust sample rate based on traffic; keep all errors, sample successes by volume.

Tail sampling is the modern best practice for production; the OpenTelemetry Collector has a built-in tail sampling processor. It costs more memory (buffer all spans until trace completes), but the signal-to-noise of stored traces is dramatically better.

USE method, RED method, Four Golden Signals: which for what?

Three operational frameworks; each fits a different layer.

  • USE (Brendan Gregg): Utilization, Saturation, Errors. For resources (CPU, memory, disk, network). “Is the resource near full? Is anything queued? Are there errors at this resource?”
  • RED (Tom Wilkie): Rate, Errors, Duration. For services. “How many requests per second? What fraction failed? How long did they take?”
  • Four Golden Signals (Google SRE book): Latency, Traffic, Errors, Saturation. Hybrid — like RED + saturation. The default for end-user-facing services where saturation matters as much as request quality.

In practice, instrument all of them at the right layer: USE for nodes & resources, RED for services, golden signals at the user-facing edge. Each is a checklist — they don’t replace each other.

The interview-relevant point: knowing the names and provenance signals you’ve internalized the SRE literature, not just “we have dashboards.”

SLI, SLO, SLA: precise definitions and a worked example.
  • SLI (Service Level Indicator): a measurement — a metric you compute, e.g., “fraction of HTTP requests returning 2xx in <500ms”.
  • SLO (Service Level Objective): a target — an internal goal, e.g., “SLI > 99.9% over a 30-day rolling window”.
  • SLA (Service Level Agreement): a contract — what you owe customers if you miss, e.g., “refund 10% of monthly bill if availability < 99.5%”.

SLO > SLA always, by margin. SLO is your internal early-warning; SLA is your “we owe money” threshold.

The math of an SLI: good_events / total_events. Good events: 2xx within latency budget. Total: all requests. Computed over the SLO window (28 or 30 days are common).

Example: “99.9% of API requests succeed within 300ms over a 28-day window.” If you served 1M requests, you’re allowed 1000 bad ones (the error budget). Burn the budget faster than 1000/28d? You’re burning > 1x and need to slow feature work.

Probing: “What happens if you’re burning the error budget too fast?” The SRE answer: feature work freezes; team focuses on reliability work; restart features once budget recovers. This is the explicit reliability-vs-velocity tradeoff that distinguishes mature engineering culture.
What is an error budget and burn-rate alert?

An error budget = (1 − SLO) × total events. SLO of 99.9% with 1M events = 1000 errors allowed. The budget gives you a quantified license to fail.

A burn-rate alert fires when you’re consuming the budget faster than the SLO allows. If your 28-day budget is 1000 errors and you’ve burned 100 in the last hour, you’re at 100× the sustainable rate — clear emergency, page someone.

Multi-window multi-burn-rate (MWMBR), per the Google SRE workbook: a 1-hour window at >14.4× burn rate fires fast (catch sudden outages); a 6-hour window at >6× burn rate fires slower (catch slow-burn issues). The combination dramatically reduces both false positives and false negatives compared to threshold alerts.

# Prometheus burn-rate alert (sketch)
alert: HighErrorBudgetBurn
expr: |
  (
    sum(rate(http_errors_total[1h])) / sum(rate(http_requests_total[1h]))
  ) > 14.4 * (1 - 0.999)
for: 5m
Why do burn-rate alerts outperform threshold alerts?

A threshold alert fires when error rate exceeds X% (e.g., 1%). Problems:

  • False positives: a momentary 1.1% blip during a deploy fires the page; nothing is actually wrong with the service’s SLO.
  • False negatives: a sustained 0.9% error rate doesn’t fire but quietly burns through your monthly error budget in a week.
  • Detached from the customer-experience target: you’re alerting on a number nobody on the business side cares about.

Burn-rate alerts tie directly to the SLO. They fire when your actual customer-experience commitment is at risk. Slow burns get a slow alert; sudden disasters get an immediate page. Combined with multi-window detection, the false-positive and false-negative rates both drop materially.

The cultural shift: from “wake up if any number is weird” to “wake up if we’re going to miss our SLO.” The latter is what your CEO actually cares about; everything else is noise to the on-call.

What's the difference between “blameless” and “consequence-free” postmortems?

Blameless postmortem = the incident analysis focuses on systemic causes, not individuals. The premise: humans operating in good faith made decisions that seemed correct given what they knew; the system allowed those decisions to cause harm. Fix the system.

This is not “consequence-free”:

  • If someone bypassed established review process to ship the change that broke prod, that’s a behavioral issue. Process exists for reasons; willful violation is accountability-relevant.
  • If someone violated a security policy (committed secrets, granted unauthorized access) that’s separately handleable.
  • Patterns of repeated similar errors from one person may indicate training need or capability gap.

The blameless principle: during the analysis, you don’t shame, fire, or threaten. People speak openly because they trust they won’t be punished for honesty. Outside the analysis, normal performance management still applies for the rare cases where it’s warranted.

The benefit: organizations that conflate “blameless” with “consequence-free” eventually rot — people game the system. Organizations that confuse “accountable” with “blame” get cover-ups and silent failures. The line is real and worth defending.

What is “toil” and what is the SRE 50% rule?

From the Google SRE book: toil is operational work that is manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical, devoid of enduring value, and that scales linearly with service growth. Restarting a flaky service. Manually approving routine deploys. Filing tickets to clean up old logs.

The opposite of toil is engineering work — building automation that eliminates future toil, designing reliability improvements, capacity planning. Engineering work compounds; toil doesn’t.

The 50% rule: SRE teams should spend at most 50% of their time on toil. The other half on engineering. If toil exceeds 50%, the team is unsustainable — they’ll never have time to fix the things causing the toil. Action required: hire, automate, or push toil back to the dev team that owns it.

The interview signal: a candidate who frames their work as “here’s the toil I eliminated and the engineering ratio I tracked” is operating at staff/principal level. A candidate who’s never measured this is more likely to be a senior individual contributor.

What's the difference between observability and monitoring?

Monitoring: pre-defined dashboards and alerts on metrics you knew to track in advance. Answers known questions: “is the service up? Is latency in band?”

Observability: the ability to ask new questions about your system without re-deploying. Answers questions you didn’t anticipate: “why are tenant X’s requests slow specifically when feature flag Y is on?”

The technical distinction: monitoring is about pre-aggregated metrics; observability requires high-cardinality, high-dimensional data — events with many fields you can pivot on. Wide structured logs, traces with rich attributes, exemplars linking metrics to traces.

Tools shape the boundary: Datadog, New Relic, Honeycomb, and Splunk Observability Cloud lean “observability” (event-based). Prometheus + Grafana lean “monitoring” (metrics-first). Modern stacks blend: metrics for alerting, traces & logs for investigation, exemplars to bridge.

The Charity Majors phrasing: “monitoring is for known unknowns; observability is for unknown unknowns.” A good interview answer adapts that framing.

What do you put in logs vs metrics vs traces?

Each pillar has a budget: storage, query, ingestion. Misuse one and you’ll either pay too much or lose the signal.

  • Metrics: aggregatable, low-cardinality, fast to query, cheap. Use for: rates, counts, distributions you alert on. Don’t put: user IDs, request IDs, anything with cardinality > ~1000.
  • Logs: high-cardinality structured records with full context. Use for: business events, errors with full context, security audit trails. Don’t put: every request body (storage death), credentials, PII without redaction.
  • Traces: causal request paths with timing per span. Use for: debugging cross-service latency, understanding fan-out, identifying which downstream broke. Don’t use: as a primary log source (sampling drops most traces).

Cross-correlate: every log line should carry the trace_id of the inflight request. Every metric should support exemplars (a single trace_id linked to a metric data point). The result: alert on metric → pivot to logs/traces in one click. The integrated observability platform’s key value-add.

§ 07  Transaction isolation

Anomalies you defend against, by name.

Isolation is the “I” in ACID and the part most engineers wave at without truly understanding. The interview-relevant material: which anomalies each level prevents, what your engine’s default actually is (it’s usually weaker than you think), and the cases where Snapshot Isolation is almost Serializable but not quite.

Concept · The anomaly ladder The anomalies, weakest to strongest: dirty write (one transaction overwrites another’s uncommitted data) → dirty read (read uncommitted data) → non-repeatable read (re-reading a row, you see a different value) → phantom read (re-running a range query, you see new rows) → lost update (two transactions read-modify-write, one overwrites the other) → read skew (you read inconsistent state across rows) → write skew (concurrent writes each safe in isolation, jointly violating an invariant).

ANSI defined four levels (Read Uncommitted, Read Committed, Repeatable Read, Serializable) but the meaning of each varies dramatically per engine. Postgres’ Repeatable Read is Snapshot Isolation; Oracle’s Serializable is also SI; only Postgres SSI gives you true Serializable cheaply. Read the docs of your specific engine.
Define the seven anomalies precisely.
  • Dirty write: T1 writes X. T2 (concurrently) writes X without seeing T1’s value or rolling it back atomically. Mostly impossible with row-level locking; almost no engine actually allows this.
  • Dirty read: T1 writes X. T2 reads X before T1 commits. T1 rolls back; T2 has read a value that never existed.
  • Non-repeatable read (fuzzy read): T1 reads X = 5. Concurrently, T2 commits X = 7. T1 reads X again, gets 7. Two reads in the same transaction return different values for the same row.
  • Phantom read: T1 runs SELECT * WHERE x > 10, gets 3 rows. T2 inserts a 4th row matching the predicate and commits. T1 re-runs the query, gets 4 rows. New rows appeared.
  • Lost update: T1 reads X = 5; T2 reads X = 5. T1 writes X = 6. T2 writes X = 6 (instead of 7). T2’s intended increment is lost. Read-modify-write race.
  • Read skew: T1 reads row A, sees value 100. T2 commits a transfer (A becomes 50, B becomes 150). T1 reads B, sees 150. T1 has now seen state from before AND after T2 — inconsistent.
  • Write skew: Two transactions each read a value, each decide their own write is OK because the predicate held. Together, the writes violate a global invariant. Classic example: two doctors both go off-call concurrently, each seeing the other still on-call (see next Q&A).

Knowing the names lets you precisely describe the bug your isolation level allows. “We had a write-skew bug in the on-call scheduler” is a vastly more credible report than “something concurrent broke.”

What is write skew, and why does Snapshot Isolation allow it?

The canonical example: an on-call rotation requires at least one doctor to be on duty at all times. Two doctors, A and B, each request to go off-call simultaneously. Under Snapshot Isolation, both transactions:

  1. Read “is anyone else on-call?” — both see the other still on-call.
  2. Verify the invariant holds (yes, the other is still on-call).
  3. Set themselves off-call.
  4. Commit.

Each transaction is correct in its snapshot. But the combined effect violates “at least one on-call.” Each saw the predicate hold in its snapshot; in reality, both committed and the invariant broke.

SI doesn’t catch this because there’s no direct write conflict — A wrote A’s row, B wrote B’s row. Different rows, no overlap from MVCC’s perspective. The conflict is in the predicate they both checked, which both saw to hold.

Real-world incidence:

  • On-call schedulers, doctor coverage.
  • Inventory: “don’t oversell” with multiple concurrent purchases of last-N items.
  • Banking: enforcing “account balance never goes negative” across concurrent withdrawals.
  • Booking: “one user can hold at most N reservations”.

The defense: explicit row locking on the predicate (SELECT ... FOR UPDATE), Serializable Snapshot Isolation (Postgres SSI), or moving the constraint to a uniqueness constraint at the DB level.

Probing: “Snapshot Isolation gives you Serializable, right?” If a candidate says yes, ask about doctors going off-call. The right answer covers SI’s write-skew gap and what you do about it.
How does MVCC implement Snapshot Isolation, and why is it the modern default?

MVCC (Multi-Version Concurrency Control): each row carries a creation transaction ID and (when overwritten) a deletion transaction ID. Writes don’t overwrite in place; they create a new version. Each transaction reads the version that was current when it started (its snapshot).

Properties:

  • Readers don’t block writers; writers don’t block readers. The killer feature. Long-running reads coexist with high-throughput writes.
  • Each transaction sees a consistent snapshot — the database state as of its start. No phantom reads, no fuzzy reads within the transaction.
  • Garbage — old versions accumulate, must be reclaimed (Postgres VACUUM, Oracle undo segments, MySQL InnoDB rollback segments).

SI is the natural pairing: each transaction reads its snapshot, write conflicts on the same row are detected at commit (first-committer-wins, or SI variants block until commit). Most modern databases implement SI: Postgres, Oracle, SQL Server (with READ_COMMITTED_SNAPSHOT), MySQL InnoDB, MongoDB WiredTiger, CockroachDB, Spanner.

The cost: storage for old versions; vacuum/cleanup overhead; the write-skew gap. The benefit: single-writer / many-reader workloads scale beautifully without the lock convoy of pessimistic 2PL.

Postgres SSI (Serializable Snapshot Isolation): what does it add to SI?

SSI, designed by Cahill et al. (SIGMOD 2008) and shipped in Postgres 9.1, gives you true Serializable on top of Snapshot Isolation. The trick: detect dangerous read-write dependency cycles at runtime; abort one transaction in the cycle.

How: Postgres tracks read locks (in addition to write locks — just metadata about what each transaction read). When a commit would create an SI anomaly — a read-write dependency cycle — one transaction is aborted with could not serialize access due to read/write dependencies among transactions.

This catches write skew. The doctors-going-off-call scenario: both transactions read each other’s rows (the predicate check), then write. SSI detects the rw-dependency cycle, aborts one. Application sees a serialization failure and retries.

Cost:

  • More overhead than SI (read tracking).
  • Spurious aborts — SSI can abort a transaction that would have committed under a true Serializable schedule. Defensible, conservative.
  • Application must handle serialization_failure by retrying.

Why it’s special: Postgres is the only widely-deployed open-source database with cheap true Serializable. MySQL’s Serializable is much heavier (uses next-key locks, lock-everything-you-touch). Oracle’s Serializable is actually SI. Use Postgres SSI when correctness matters; pay the modest performance cost.

What's the actual default isolation level on Postgres, MySQL, Oracle, SQL Server, DynamoDB?
  • Postgres: Read Committed. Snapshot Isolation available via SET TRANSACTION ISOLATION LEVEL REPEATABLE READ; SSI via ... SERIALIZABLE.
  • MySQL InnoDB: Repeatable Read. MySQL’s RR uses Snapshot Isolation + gap locks (next-key locks) which prevent phantoms in range scans. Stricter than Postgres’ RR (which is plain SI; phantoms via predicate locking only at SSI level).
  • Oracle: Read Committed. “Serializable” is actually Snapshot Isolation (allows write skew).
  • SQL Server: Read Committed by default; READ_COMMITTED_SNAPSHOT setting flips to MVCC-based RC. True Snapshot via SNAPSHOT level. True Serializable via lock-based approach (similar to MySQL RR).
  • DynamoDB: per-item operations are eventually-consistent reads by default; strongly consistent reads are an explicit per-call flag. Transactions (TransactWriteItems / TransactGetItems) provide ACID across multiple items in a single region.

The trap: assuming “ACID” means “Serializable.” It rarely does. Read Committed lets you observe non-repeatable reads, lost updates, write skew — all the way down. Always state the level explicitly when discussing transactional correctness.

Interview gotcha: “Postgres is fully ACID” is incomplete. The default is Read Committed; Serializable is opt-in. Saying “Postgres at default isolation” means lost updates and non-repeatable reads are possible. Knowing this distinguishes senior-deep from senior-broad.
Why does “Repeatable Read” mean different things on different engines?

The ANSI standard defined Repeatable Read as “prevents non-repeatable reads”, with no MVCC concept. Engines implemented it differently:

  • Postgres RR = Snapshot Isolation. Prevents non-repeatable reads, phantoms, dirty reads. Allows write skew.
  • MySQL InnoDB RR = SI + next-key locks for range scans. Stronger than Postgres RR — prevents most phantoms via gap locking. Cost: gap locks reduce concurrency, can deadlock easily.
  • SQL Server RR = lock-based. Acquires shared locks on read, holds them to commit. No MVCC by default. Bad concurrency.
  • Oracle doesn’t have RR — jumps from RC to (SI-flavored) Serializable.

The takeaway: when discussing isolation across engines, never use the ANSI label alone. Always say what it concretely guarantees on that engine. The 1995 Berenson et al. paper (A Critique of ANSI SQL Isolation Levels) is the canonical reference for why the ANSI definitions are inadequate.

Optimistic vs pessimistic concurrency control: when which?

Pessimistic (locking): acquire locks on read/write; conflicts block. Used by SQL Server lock-based RR/Serializable, MySQL Serializable, classic 2PL implementations. Trade-offs: predictable behavior, but lock convoys and deadlocks under contention; readers block writers (often).

Optimistic (validate-at-commit): proceed without locking; check at commit whether someone else changed your read set. If so, abort and retry. Used by SI / SSI implementations, application-level optimistic locking via version columns, distributed systems (CockroachDB, Spanner partly). Trade-offs: high throughput under low contention; abort-and-retry storms under high contention.

Picker:

  • Low contention (most workloads): optimistic. SI, MVCC, app-level version-column. Fast, no blocking.
  • High contention on specific rows (inventory of a hot product, account balance): pessimistic via SELECT ... FOR UPDATE on the row. Block instead of abort-retry.
  • Long-running computations with many reads: pessimistic shared locks help avoid mid-flight changes; consider a snapshot DB instead.

Application-level optimistic locking pattern:

UPDATE account
   SET balance = 100, version = version + 1
 WHERE id = '42' AND version = 17;
-- if 0 rows updated, retry from re-read
What is the “phantom read” specifically, and why does it matter for Repeatable Read?

A phantom read involves new rows appearing in a re-executed range query. Even under Repeatable Read (SI flavor), a transaction can’t guarantee that a range it reads stays the same set of rows — another transaction can insert a row matching the predicate.

Why this is more than academic: anything that says “exactly these rows exist matching X” is at risk. Counts, aggregates over a range, “the top 10 customers by spend this month.”

Defenses:

  • Postgres RR (SI): doesn’t prevent phantoms in writes based on a phantom-prone predicate. Use SSI to catch.
  • MySQL InnoDB RR: next-key locks (gap locks) prevent inserts into the read range until commit. Cost: reduced concurrency.
  • True Serializable (Postgres SSI, MySQL Serializable, SQL Server lock-based): phantoms not possible.
  • Application: if you only care that your writes are based on a stable set, lock the range explicitly (FOR UPDATE) or use a unique index that rejects conflicts.

The interview-relevant fact: ANSI Repeatable Read on paper allows phantoms. Whether your engine’s RR allows them depends on the engine.

What's the difference between read skew and write skew?

Both are anomalies allowed by Read Committed and (write skew only) by Snapshot Isolation. They differ in whether the transaction itself wrote anything inconsistent.

  • Read skew: a read-only transaction sees inconsistent state. T1 reads from snapshot S1; T2 (concurrent transfer) commits, changing two related rows; T1’s next read reflects only one of the changes — T1’s view is internally inconsistent. Solved by SI (snapshot is fixed for T1).
  • Write skew: two transactions each read something, each write based on what they read; the writes individually look fine but jointly violate an invariant. Solved by Serializable / SSI / explicit predicate locking.

Read Committed allows both. SI fixes read skew. Only true Serializable fixes write skew.

The naming is confusing because both are called “skew” but they’re different beasts. Read skew is about a single transaction’s view; write skew is about multi-transaction outcomes. Knowing which one you’re defending against is the staff-level signal.

How do you implement “don't oversell” correctly under concurrency?

Inventory of N items, many concurrent buyers. Classic write-skew scenario at SI.

Wrong: SELECT count FROM inventory; if count > 0 then UPDATE inventory SET count = count - 1. Two concurrent buyers both see count=1, both decrement, one ends up at 0 and one at -1. Oversold.

Right options:

  • Atomic decrement with check: UPDATE inventory SET count = count - 1 WHERE id = X AND count > 0 RETURNING count. Single statement; the engine’s row lock during the update prevents the race. Most general, works on any RDBMS.
  • SELECT FOR UPDATE: SELECT count FROM inventory WHERE id = X FOR UPDATE in a transaction; check; update. Pessimistic but explicit.
  • Per-item rows: represent each unit of inventory as a row; selling = updating a row from available to reserved. Concurrent buyers race on different rows; first to reserve wins. Scales but uses more storage.
  • Serializable isolation: declare and forget; rely on the DB to detect & abort. Works but increases retry-storm under heavy contention.

The atomic-decrement-with-check is the production sweet spot for most cases. It’s O(1), works at any isolation level, and is easy to reason about.

What is consistency in the CAP sense vs the ACID sense?

Two completely different things sharing a word. Confusion is rampant.

  • ACID Consistency (the “C”): the database moves from one valid state to another, respecting integrity constraints (foreign keys, unique constraints, check constraints, triggers). It’s about application-defined invariants.
  • CAP Consistency: linearizability. Every read sees the latest committed write across all nodes. It’s about distributed-system observable state.

A single-node Postgres at Read Committed is fully ACID-consistent (constraints enforced) but not necessarily CAP-consistent (with replicas, async replication breaks linearizability). A distributed eventually-consistent system can be CAP-inconsistent yet always valid against its own constraints.

When discussing “consistency,” always disambiguate. The Brewer CAP theorem “consistency” is linearizability; the “C” in ACID is a much weaker, application-defined property. The Aphyr / Kleppmann literature is rigorous about this; most blog posts aren’t.

How do you reason about isolation in a distributed transaction (cross-shard, cross-DC)?

Single-node isolation extends differently to distributed setups. Most modern distributed DBs offer:

  • Serializable + Linearizable (Spanner, CockroachDB, FaunaDB): the gold standard. Spanner uses TrueTime (synchronized clocks ± bounded uncertainty) to provide external consistency. CockroachDB uses HLC (hybrid logical clocks) + retries. Cost: cross-region latency for commits.
  • Snapshot Isolation across shards (CockroachDB SI mode, YugabyteDB): no write skew within a transaction; faster than full Serializable.
  • Eventual / causal (DynamoDB, Cassandra, Cosmos DB at lighter levels): per-item or per-partition guarantees; multi-row consistency requires app-level reasoning.

The general lesson: “ACID transactions” in a distributed system is a non-trivial claim — ask which level, across what scope (shard, region, global). DynamoDB’s TransactWriteItems are ACID across items in one region; cross-region is not transactional.

For interviews: if you’re proposing a distributed transaction, justify the scope. “Single-region, single-shard” is the cheap path; “multi-region, strongly consistent” is Spanner-level engineering and Spanner-level latency cost.

§ 08  Authentication & authorization

Two words people confuse, ten years of mistakes.

Authentication asks who are you; authorization asks what may you do. The first is about identity verification; the second is about policy. Mixing them up is the source of half the access-control bugs in production. The other half come from JWT mishandling. This section is opinionated: implicit OAuth flow is dead, JWT signature validation is non-negotiable, and ReBAC is the future for SaaS.

Concept · The clean separation AuthN (authentication): prove identity. Result: a verified principal (user, service). Mechanisms: passwords, MFA, social login, mTLS, SPIFFE identities, OIDC.

AuthZ (authorization): enforce policy. Result: allow / deny on a specific action against a specific resource by a specific principal. Models: RBAC (roles), ABAC (attributes), ReBAC (relationships), policy languages (OPA/Rego, Cedar). Multi-tenant SaaS adds tenant scoping as a non-negotiable axis on every check.

Confusing the two leads to mistakes like “the JWT proves the user is allowed” (it doesn’t — it proves identity; the policy engine decides allowed). Keep the layers separate.
Session-based vs token-based auth: trade-offs.

Session-based (cookie + server session store): server stores session data (user ID, expiry, scopes); client gets an opaque session ID in a cookie. Every request looks up the session.

  • + Server-side revocation is trivial (delete the session row).
  • + Sensitive data lives server-side; cookie is just an ID.
  • − Stateful — need a session store (Redis, sticky sessions, or shared DB).
  • − CSRF is a real concern; need SameSite cookie + CSRF tokens or origin checks.

Token-based (JWT or opaque token): server issues a self-contained token; client sends it on each request (typically Authorization: Bearer ...). Server validates the token cryptographically.

  • + Stateless — any node can validate without shared state. Good for horizontal scale, microservices.
  • + Tokens contain claims, useful for downstream services.
  • − Hard to revoke before expiry (JWT has no built-in revocation).
  • − Token theft = full access until expiry; refresh-token rotation is mandatory mitigation.

Modern picker: B2B / SPA + microservices → token-based with short-lived access + refresh rotation. Traditional web app, single trust boundary → session cookies are still excellent and simpler. Don’t use JWT just because it’s trendy.

OAuth2 flows: which is right for which client?

OAuth2 (RFC 6749, 2012) defines several grant types; modern guidance has narrowed to a few:

  • Authorization Code with PKCE (RFC 7636): the modern default for SPAs, native apps, mobile, and confidential web apps. PKCE replaces client secret for public clients.
  • Client Credentials: machine-to-machine. Service A authenticates as itself to call Service B’s API. No user involved.
  • Device Code (RFC 8628): for input-constrained devices (TVs, CLI tools). User authorizes on a phone; device polls token endpoint.
  • Refresh Token: not a grant per se; the long-lived companion to short-lived access tokens. Rotated on use (RFC 6819 best practice).

Dead and gone:

  • Implicit flow: deprecated in OAuth 2.1. Tokens in URL fragments leaked everywhere; no refresh token possible. Use Auth Code + PKCE for SPAs.
  • Resource Owner Password Credentials (ROPC): the user gives their password directly to the client. Only acceptable in legacy migrations; OAuth 2.1 deprecates it.

If a candidate proposes Implicit or ROPC for a new design, that’s a teachable moment. The current best practice is RFC 9700 (OAuth 2.0 Security Best Current Practice) and the in-progress OAuth 2.1 consolidation.

Probing: “What does PKCE protect against?” The authorization-code-interception attack on public clients (mobile, SPA): a malicious app intercepts the redirect URI and captures the code; without PKCE, it could exchange the code for tokens. PKCE’s code-verifier ties the code to the original requester.
OIDC vs OAuth2: what does OIDC add?

OAuth2 is an authorization framework: it grants access tokens for resources. OAuth2 by itself does not define how to identify the user — the access token is opaque to the client.

OpenID Connect (OIDC) is an authentication layer on top of OAuth2. It adds:

  • ID Token: a JWT with standardized identity claims (sub, email, name, aud, iss, exp, iat, nonce). Lets the client verify who the user is.
  • UserInfo endpoint: an HTTP endpoint to fetch additional user claims with the access token.
  • Discovery: a well-known URL (/.well-known/openid-configuration) describing the OP’s endpoints and capabilities.
  • JWKS (JSON Web Key Set): a JSON document publishing the OP’s public signing keys; clients fetch and cache this to verify ID Tokens.

The two-token model:

  • ID token: for the client. Proves who the user is. Don’t send to APIs.
  • Access token: for the resource API. Proves the user authorized this client to call the API. Don’t parse it for identity claims (it’s opaque to the client per OAuth semantics).

Common mistake: using the ID token as a bearer token to APIs. ID tokens are not meant for API authorization; access tokens are. Conversely, parsing access tokens for user info is fragile (the API may rotate to opaque tokens; the client breaks). Use UserInfo or the ID token claims for identity.

JWT structure: what's actually inside, and how is it validated?

A JWT is three Base64URL-encoded parts separated by dots: header.payload.signature.

  • Header: JSON with alg (signing algorithm: RS256, ES256, HS256) and kid (key ID, identifying which key to use).
  • Payload: JSON claims. Standard ones (RFC 7519): iss (issuer), sub (subject/user), aud (audience), exp (expiration), nbf (not before), iat (issued at), jti (JWT ID).
  • Signature: HMAC or RSA/ECDSA over header.payload.

Validation (every step required):

  1. Parse structure; reject malformed.
  2. Look up the key (typically by kid from JWKS).
  3. Verify the signature using the issuer’s public key (asymmetric) or shared secret (HS256).
  4. Verify alg: must match the expected algorithm. Reject alg=none. Reject mismatches with your expected alg (an HS256 token verified as RS256 with a public key as the secret can be forged).
  5. Verify exp, nbf, iat with reasonable clock skew.
  6. Verify iss matches your trusted issuer.
  7. Verify aud matches your service.
  8. (For ID tokens) verify the nonce matches the one your client included in the auth request.

Skip any step and you have a security bug. Use a vetted library (jose, nimbus-jose-jwt, jsonwebtoken). Don’t roll your own.

// Java with nimbus-jose-jwt
JWKSource<SecurityContext> keys = JWKSourceBuilder.create(jwksUrl).build();
DefaultJWTProcessor<SecurityContext> jwt = new DefaultJWTProcessor<>();
jwt.setJWSKeySelector(new JWSVerificationKeySelector<>(JWSAlgorithm.RS256, keys));
jwt.setJWTClaimsSetVerifier(new DefaultJWTClaimsVerifier<>(
  new JWTClaimsSet.Builder().issuer(expectedIss).audience(expectedAud).build(),
  Set.of("sub", "exp", "iat")));
What is the “alg=none” attack, and how does the asymmetric/symmetric confusion work?

Two classic JWT attacks targeting validation flaws:

alg=none attack: the JWT spec allows alg: "none", meaning “no signature.” A naive validator that takes alg from the header and dispatches to the named algorithm will accept any unsigned token. Attacker forges a token with alg=none, no signature, arbitrary claims; validator accepts.

HS/RS confusion: server expects RS256 (verified with public key). Attacker creates a token with alg=HS256; validator naively uses the public key as the HMAC shared secret. Public keys are public, so attacker can sign anything that “verifies” with HS256.

Both attacks have been found in major libraries (jsonwebtoken in Node, python-jwt, others) over the years. The fix in modern libraries: require an explicit expected algorithm at validation time. Don’t trust alg from the header to decide what to verify with.

Defenses to verify in your code:

  • Reject alg=none always.
  • Verify against an explicit alg (RS256), not whatever the header claims.
  • For RS256, ensure the “secret” passed to verify is the public key, not parsed in a way that allows symmetric usage.
Gotcha: “We use JWT” without specifying signature algorithm and validation library is a yellow flag. The right answer specifies RS256 or ES256 (asymmetric, scales across services), JWKS for key distribution, and a vetted library.
Why use JWT vs opaque tokens?

JWT (self-contained): claims live in the token; resource servers verify signature and trust claims. No central lookup; any service can validate independently with the public key.

  • + Stateless, scalable, reduces auth-server load.
  • + Carries claims downstream without extra lookups.
  • − Hard to revoke before expiry (token blacklist or JTI revocation list undermines stateless benefit).
  • − Larger than opaque tokens (KB-sized vs ~32 bytes).
  • − Stale claims — user’s permissions changed, but token has old claims until expiry.

Opaque tokens: a random string; resource server calls the auth server’s introspection endpoint (RFC 7662) to validate & resolve to claims.

  • + Trivial revocation (auth server invalidates the row).
  • + Always fresh — introspection returns current claims.
  • + Tiny.
  • − Every request has an introspection cost (mitigated by short caching).

Hybrid (modern best practice):

  • JWT access tokens with short TTL (minutes).
  • Opaque refresh tokens (hours-to-weeks) for renewal.
  • Auth server can revoke by invalidating the refresh token; access tokens expire quickly anyway.
  • For high-stakes operations, validate against a revocation list or call introspection.

Auth0, Okta, Cognito all default to this pattern. The trade-off is roughly: how much do you value revocation latency vs introspection cost?

Refresh token rotation and reuse detection: why mandatory?

A long-lived refresh token in the wrong hands = effective full account compromise. Two complementary defenses:

Rotation: every time a refresh token is exchanged for a new access token, also issue a new refresh token; invalidate the old one. The token in use is always fresh.

Reuse detection: if an old (already-used) refresh token is presented, that’s evidence of compromise — either a legitimate client retried with stale state, or an attacker is using a stolen old token. Standard response: invalidate the entire token family for that user, force re-login.

Implementation:

  • Each refresh token has a family ID + sequence number.
  • On rotation, issue token with same family + new sequence; mark old sequence used.
  • On presentation of a token whose sequence is already marked used: kill the family, force re-auth, alert security.

This is OAuth 2.1 / RFC 9700 best current practice. Auth0 and most modern auth providers implement it. If you’re building auth and not doing rotation + reuse detection, you’re shipping a footgun.

RBAC vs ABAC vs ReBAC: when which?

Three authorization models, increasing in expressiveness and complexity:

  • RBAC (Role-Based): users have roles; roles have permissions. “Admins can delete users.” Simple, mature, well-supported. Breaks down when you need conditional permissions or relationship-based access.
  • ABAC (Attribute-Based): policies evaluate attributes of subject, resource, action, context. “User can edit a doc if user.dept == doc.dept and time is business-hours.” Expressive; policy-language-driven (XACML historically; modern: OPA/Rego, AWS Cedar).
  • ReBAC (Relationship-Based): explicit relationships drive access. “Alice can edit doc D because Alice is owner of folder F which contains D.” Google Zanzibar (the system behind Drive, Calendar, Cloud) is the seminal paper. Open-source implementations: OpenFGA (Auth0), SpiceDB (AuthZed), Permify.

Picker:

  • Simple admin-vs-user product, few permission types: RBAC. Don’t over-engineer.
  • Complex conditional rules, regulatory: ABAC with OPA. Centralized policies, decoupled from app code.
  • Document/folder hierarchies, sharing, multi-tenant SaaS with collaboration: ReBAC. Zanzibar-style is the right shape; the alternative is reinventing it badly.

The trend in 2026: ReBAC is winning for new SaaS with sharing semantics. Notion, Figma, Linear-style apps need relationship reasoning; encoding it in RBAC requires explosive role enumeration. ABAC + OPA remains strong for backend platforms with heavy compliance.

Multi-tenant authorization: what's the rule you can't break?

The rule: every authorization check includes the tenant scope. There is no “does this user have permission?” without “in this tenant.”

The class of bug this prevents: cross-tenant data leakage. User Alice from Tenant A has a session; she crafts a request for resource ID 42 from Tenant B; if the AuthZ check is “is Alice an owner of resource 42?” without tenant context, Alice may legitimately own a resource 42 in her tenant, and the check passes for the wrong row.

Defenses, layered:

  • Resource lookups always tenant-scoped: SELECT ... WHERE id = ? AND tenant_id = ?. The DB query itself filters; missing the tenant clause is a bug visible at code review.
  • Row-level security (Postgres RLS, MySQL views): enforce at the DB. Even if app code forgets, the DB rejects.
  • Per-tenant credentials in connection: the connection itself is scoped to a tenant.
  • Object IDs that include tenant: ten_acme/doc_42. Cross-tenant references can’t be guessed; mismatched tenant in the ID is rejected.
  • Centralized AuthZ middleware: every API call goes through a check that pulls (user, tenant) from the auth context and rejects mismatches.

Cross-tenant data leakage is one of the most damaging classes of SaaS bug. Prevention is structural — not “remember to add the where clause.”

Probing: “How would you prevent IDOR (Insecure Direct Object Reference) in a multi-tenant API?” The right answer involves multiple defenses: AuthZ check on every read, opaque IDs, RLS, audit. The wrong answer is “we check permissions in the controller.”
Service-to-service auth: SPIFFE, mTLS, signed JWTs.

Three approaches for service-to-service identity inside a fleet:

  • mTLS (mutual TLS): both sides present certificates; both validate. Identity = certificate Subject Alternative Name. Strong, well-understood, but cert lifecycle (issuance, rotation, revocation) is the work.
  • SPIFFE / SPIRE: standardized identity framework (CNCF). Each workload gets a SPIFFE ID (a URI like spiffe://prod.example.com/ns/billing/sa/api) and a short-lived X.509 SVID (Secure Verifiable Identity Document) or JWT-SVID. SPIRE is the reference implementation; rotates certs every ~10 minutes automatically.
  • Signed JWTs (workload-issued): each service has a signing key; calls signed; receivers verify. Used in service mesh sidecars (Istio, Linkerd). Often ride on top of mTLS for transport security.

Service mesh (Istio, Linkerd, Consul Connect) typically handles all three transparently. The sidecar injects mTLS, propagates JWT-encoded principal info upstream, exposes identity to policy for AuthZ. The dev experience: just use HTTP; the mesh does identity.

For non-mesh deployments (or as a pre-mesh step), SPIRE + cert-manager is the modern self-managed answer. AWS / GCP / Azure all expose IAM-rooted identity (IRSA on EKS, Workload Identity on GKE, Managed Identity on Azure) for cloud-native equivalents.

Why are short-lived credentials better than long-lived ones?

Long-lived credentials (static API keys, certs valid for years, AWS IAM access keys) have multiple bad properties:

  • Compromise has long blast radius: if a key leaks, attacker has access until you notice and rotate. Rotation often takes weeks at organizational pace.
  • Rotation is painful: humans coordinate, deploys lag, things break.
  • No automatic expiry: a forgotten cred lingers forever in CI logs, env vars, GitHub history.

Short-lived credentials (minutes to hours):

  • Compromise window is bounded by TTL. Even an undetected leak has limited damage.
  • Rotation is the normal path — not an event.
  • Issuance is gated by something (workload identity, SAML token, TPM-attested device) — the gating mechanism is what you protect.

Modern patterns: AWS STS AssumeRole (1h default, 12h max); GCP Workload Identity; HashiCorp Vault dynamic secrets (DB credentials issued per-request). All shift the trust to a centrally-managed issuer with short outputs.

The cultural piece: short-lived creds force you to engineer for rotation. Once rotation is a non-event, you actually get the security benefit. Long-lived creds let you defer rotation forever; the “deferred work” is also “deferred safety.”

What's the right way to do API key authentication?

API keys are simple bearer credentials. Trivial to misuse. Right way:

  • Issue from a central place (your account / dashboard); never let users invent them.
  • Store hashed server-side (like passwords). Argon2id, bcrypt, or HMAC-SHA256 with a peppered secret. On request, hash the presented key and compare. If your DB leaks, attacker has hashes, not usable keys.
  • Show prefix only in UI (sk_live_abc…); never echo the full key after issuance.
  • Allow rotation: support multiple active keys per identity so customers can rotate without downtime.
  • Scope keys: each key has a permission set (read-only, billing-read, etc.) — principle of least privilege. Stripe’s restricted keys are the canonical example.
  • IP allowlists / domain restrictions for high-stakes keys.
  • Audit log every use: when, from where, what action. Helps detect misuse.
  • Detect leak in public repos: partner with GitHub Secret Scanning to revoke leaked keys automatically (Stripe, AWS, Google all do this).

Anti-patterns: keys in URL paths (logged everywhere), keys in error messages, keys with no scope, no rotation story.

§ 09  Data protection & compliance

Defense in depth, with auditors watching.

Encryption, key management, secrets handling, and the engineering-relevant slice of compliance. The interview signal: do you know which part of SOC 2 you actually touch as an engineer, do you know why HSMs exist, can you explain why “just hash the password” is incomplete in 2026? The compliance pieces are not about being a lawyer — they’re about understanding the engineering controls auditors will ask to see.

Concept · Defense in depth Encryption at rest protects data on disk. Encryption in transit protects on the wire. Encryption in use (confidential computing, TEEs) is emerging. Key management is the hard part — encryption is easy, key custody is the actual security boundary. Secrets management is key management for credentials. PII handling (tokenization, pseudonymization) reduces blast radius of a leak. Compliance frameworks bundle controls — SOC 2, HIPAA, GDPR, PCI-DSS — and require evidence that those controls operate.

The pattern: encryption everywhere, KMS-managed keys with rotation, secrets in Vault, PII minimized, audit logs immutable, and a clear data-flow diagram you can show an auditor.
Encryption at rest: AES-256, GCM vs CBC, envelope encryption.

AES-256 is the universal symmetric primitive in 2026. Quantum-safe in current public knowledge; standardized everywhere.

Mode matters — AES is a block cipher; the mode determines how blocks are chained:

  • GCM (Galois/Counter Mode): authenticated encryption with associated data (AEAD). Provides confidentiality + integrity in one operation. Default for new designs.
  • CBC: confidentiality only; needs separate MAC for integrity. Padding-oracle attacks (the famous TLS BEAST/Lucky13 attacks) bit hard. Avoid for new code.
  • CTR: confidentiality, no integrity. Use only with separate MAC.

Envelope encryption is the production pattern for at-rest:

  1. A Data Encryption Key (DEK) encrypts the actual data.
  2. The DEK is itself encrypted by a Key Encryption Key (KEK) stored in KMS.
  3. Encrypted data + encrypted DEK stored together; KEK stays in KMS.
  4. To decrypt: ask KMS to decrypt the DEK; use DEK to decrypt data; throw DEK away.

Why: KMS handles a small number of high-stakes keys; DEKs can be unique per object/row; no need to round-trip every byte through KMS. AWS KMS, GCP KMS, Vault Transit all use this. S3 SSE-KMS, EBS volume encryption, GCP Persistent Disk, Azure managed disks — envelope encryption underneath every cloud at-rest feature.

What's the difference between column-level, full-disk, and application-level encryption?
  • Full-disk / volume encryption: the storage layer encrypts. Protects against physical theft of disks. Doesn’t protect against compromised app, DB, or OS user — once the volume is mounted, data is plaintext to anyone reading it.
  • Database TDE (Transparent Data Encryption): the DB encrypts files on disk. Same threat model as full-disk — protects backups and stolen disks; not the connected user.
  • Column-level encryption: specific columns encrypted, often with a per-column or per-tenant key. The DB sees ciphertext; queries on encrypted columns are limited (equality via deterministic encryption, otherwise no search).
  • Application-level encryption: app encrypts before storing; DB never sees plaintext. Maximum protection — even a DB compromise yields ciphertext only. Most operational complexity (key access, search limitations).

Picker:

  • Compliance baseline (SOC 2, HIPAA): full-disk + TLS in transit. Necessary, not sufficient.
  • Sensitive PII / financial: column-level for the specific fields (SSN, card numbers, health records).
  • Keys-controlled-by-customer: BYOK or app-level encryption with customer-held KEK.

The trap: claiming “all data encrypted” when you mean full-disk only. An auditor asks “encrypted from whom?” The honest answer is “from someone who steals our disks.” That’s usually not the threat model that matters.

TLS 1.2 vs 1.3: what changed, and why are old cipher suites still around?

TLS 1.3 (RFC 8446, 2018) is a substantial cleanup of TLS 1.2:

  • Cipher suites simplified: only AEAD suites (AES-GCM, ChaCha20-Poly1305). No CBC, no RC4, no MD5, no static RSA key exchange. Five suites total vs dozens in TLS 1.2.
  • 1-RTT handshake by default; 0-RTT for resumption (replay risks; use carefully).
  • Forward secrecy mandatory (only ECDHE / DHE).
  • Downgrade attacks harder — ServerHello signs the version; old downgrade gadgets dead.

Why old cipher suites linger:

  • Legacy clients (older mobile, old enterprise, IoT devices) only do TLS 1.0/1.1/1.2 with older suites.
  • FIPS 140-2/140-3 compliance: validated module lists drive cipher choice in regulated environments.
  • Compatibility floors set by major customers’ environments.

Modern stance: TLS 1.3 default, TLS 1.2 with strong AEAD suites as fallback, everything older disabled. Mozilla’s TLS configuration generator and SSL Labs server test are the standard tools to audit.

HSTS (HTTP Strict Transport Security): tells browsers “always use HTTPS for this domain.” Combined with the HSTS Preload list (built into browsers), prevents downgrade-to-HTTP. Set Strict-Transport-Security: max-age=31536000; includeSubDomains; preload for production.

Certificate pinning: when is it the right tool, when is it a footgun?

Certificate pinning: clients ship with a hardcoded list of acceptable cert public keys (or fingerprints) for specific domains. Connections that don’t match are rejected, even if the cert is valid in the global PKI.

Right contexts:

  • Mobile apps calling their own backend — you control both sides; pinning blocks cert-substitution attacks even if a CA is compromised.
  • High-stakes IoT firmware updates.
  • Native desktop apps with strong identity needs.

Wrong contexts:

  • Browser-based pinning is dead. HPKP (HTTP Public Key Pinning) was deprecated in browsers (Chrome removed it in 2019). Operationally too dangerous — one cert mistake locks all your users out for the pin lifetime.
  • Apps with rapid backend cert rotation (Let’s Encrypt-style). Pin churn > security benefit.
  • Apps where cert mistakes can’t be undone via update (e.g., long-tail mobile installs).

If you pin in mobile: pin to an intermediate CA you control, not the leaf cert. Plan for at least two valid pins (current + backup). Have a tested rollback. Apple and Google both provide pinning helpers in their networking SDKs.

What does a KMS do that you couldn't do with files on disk?

A KMS (Key Management Service) is a managed service that holds cryptographic keys and performs operations with them, never exposing the key material. Examples: AWS KMS, GCP Cloud KMS, Azure Key Vault, HashiCorp Vault Transit.

Properties you don’t get from files:

  • Key material never leaves the boundary. Encrypt/decrypt/sign happen inside the KMS; only ciphertext or signature comes out. A compromised app server can’t exfiltrate keys.
  • Hardware-backed in production tiers: keys stored in HSMs (Hardware Security Modules), often FIPS 140-2 Level 3 validated. Tamper-evident, physically protected.
  • Auditable: every key use logged immutably (CloudTrail for AWS KMS). Required for many compliance regimes.
  • IAM-integrated: who can use which key for what operation, expressed in IAM policy.
  • Rotation primitive: keys can be rotated centrally; envelope encryption keeps old data decryptable while new data uses the new key.

The trade-off: every operation is an API call. Latency (~ms), cost (~per-1000-ops). Mitigated by envelope encryption (KMS only protects DEKs; data is encrypted with cached DEKs).

BYOK, HYOK, customer-managed keys: what each means and when it matters.
  • CMK / customer-managed keys (CMK): keys managed by the customer in cloud KMS, used by cloud services. Customer can rotate, audit, disable. AWS: KMS Customer Master Keys.
  • BYOK (Bring Your Own Key): customer generates the key material elsewhere (on-prem HSM, separate cloud) and imports into KMS. Cloud uses it but can’t generate it. Provides cryptographic separation; provider can’t recreate the key from nothing.
  • HYOK (Hold Your Own Key): customer holds keys in their own HSM; cloud calls out to it for every operation. Maximum control, maximum operational pain (cloud is gated on customer’s HSM availability). Rare but real for ultra-high-stakes regulated workloads.
  • External Key Manager (EKM): GCP’s managed pattern; like HYOK but with cloud-managed integration.

Picker:

  • Most SaaS: CMK is the right balance of control and operational simplicity.
  • Regulated customers (financial, healthcare, government): often demand BYOK as a contractual term.
  • HYOK / EKM: only for the most paranoid workloads where customer-held keys are non-negotiable. Paying customers will trade availability for that control.

The other axis: data residency (must stay in country X) often interplays with key residency. EU customers sometimes require keys in EU regions only.

Secrets management: Vault, Secrets Manager, dynamic secrets.

Application credentials (DB passwords, API keys, signing keys) are special-purpose secrets. The wrong place for them is environment variables baked into images or repos. The right places:

  • HashiCorp Vault: the canonical solution. Provides static secret storage (KV engine), dynamic secret generation (DB engine, AWS engine, PKI engine), encryption-as-a-service (Transit). Self-hostable; widely adopted.
  • AWS Secrets Manager / SSM Parameter Store: AWS-native. Less feature-rich than Vault but tightly IAM-integrated. SSM has free tier for non-secret config.
  • GCP Secret Manager / Azure Key Vault: parallel offerings.
  • External Secrets Operator (Kubernetes): syncs secrets from any backend into K8s Secrets, so apps just see env vars. Bridge between “secrets live in Vault” and “apps consume env vars.”

Dynamic secrets are the killer feature: instead of a static DB password used by every instance, Vault generates a unique short-lived credential per request to a DB. If a credential leaks, the blast radius is the TTL (often minutes to hours). Compromise detection and rotation become trivial.

Maturity ladder:

  • Level 0: secrets in env vars in repos. (Real, common, bad.)
  • Level 1: secrets in Secrets Manager / Vault, fetched at startup, lifecycle managed manually.
  • Level 2: secrets fetched at startup with rotation hooks, app re-reads on rotation event.
  • Level 3: dynamic secrets with TTL; app handles rotation as a normal operation.
PII handling: tokenization, format-preserving encryption, pseudonymization.

Three techniques for reducing the surface where real PII appears:

  • Tokenization: replace PII with a random token that maps to the original in a separate vault. Most systems see and store the token; only systems that need the real value (final transaction processor, customer email service) see it. Reduces compliance scope dramatically — PCI-DSS scope shrinks to the tokenization vault.
  • Format-Preserving Encryption (FPE): encrypts a value such that the ciphertext has the same format (16-digit credit card → 16-digit ciphertext). Compatible with legacy systems expecting specific formats. NIST SP 800-38G defines the algorithms (FF1, FF3-1).
  • Pseudonymization: replace identifying fields with consistent pseudonyms (e.g., user’s email replaced with deterministic hash of email + salt). Allows analytics on linked records without exposing identity. GDPR-favored technique — pseudonymized data is still personal data but lower-risk.

Common pattern: real PII lives in a small “PII vault” service; everywhere else uses tokens. Logs use pseudonyms (so logs aren’t a PII liability). Reports use aggregate or k-anonymized data. The PII vault has the strictest controls: limited access, rotated credentials, audited.

Probing: “How would you implement right-to-be-forgotten under GDPR?” Tokenization is the structural answer: deleting from the PII vault breaks the link in every system that holds tokens, without you having to coordinate deletion across dozens of systems. Without tokenization, RTBF is a multi-month engineering project per request.
SOC 2 Type II: what does engineering actually need to do?

SOC 2 (AICPA) is a US auditing framework. Type I = controls designed correctly at a point in time. Type II = controls operated effectively over a period (typically 6–12 months). Customers want Type II.

Five Trust Service Criteria; you pick which apply:

  • Security (mandatory) — protection against unauthorized access.
  • Availability — operating per agreed-upon SLAs.
  • Confidentiality — protecting confidential information.
  • Processing Integrity — data processed completely & accurately.
  • Privacy — PII handled per the privacy notice.

Engineering-touching controls (the ones auditors will sample evidence for):

  • Access reviews quarterly (who has admin to prod? Why? Still needed?)
  • MFA on all production access.
  • Encryption at rest and in transit, demonstrated.
  • Change management: every prod change has a reviewed PR + ticket + deploy log.
  • Vulnerability management: scan cadence, remediation SLAs.
  • Incident response: documented playbooks, postmortems, customer notifications.
  • Backup & DR testing: not just “we have backups” but “we tested restore last quarter.”
  • Logging & monitoring: prod logs retained, access to prod audited.
  • Vendor management: subprocessor list, vendor security reviews.

The reality: a well-engineered shop already does most of this. SOC 2 forces you to document and produce evidence. Drata, Vanta, Secureframe automate evidence collection; they’ve become standard.

HIPAA, GDPR, PCI-DSS, CCPA: the engineering-relevant differences.

Each compliance regime governs specific data with specific requirements. The engineering-relevant slice:

  • HIPAA (US, healthcare): governs PHI (Protected Health Information). Requires encryption (in transit at minimum; at rest strongly advised), access controls, audit logging, BAA (Business Associate Agreement) with vendors. AWS, GCP, Azure all have HIPAA-eligible service lists. Right-to-access exists; no “forget” right comparable to GDPR.
  • GDPR (EU): governs personal data of EU residents. Six lawful bases for processing; data subject rights (access, rectification, erasure, portability, objection); 72-hour breach notification; DPO required for many; transfers outside EU need adequacy decisions or SCCs. Right-to-be-forgotten is the engineering hardest piece.
  • PCI-DSS (industry, card payments): governs cardholder data (PAN, CVV). Strict scope: any system that touches PAN is in scope. Tokenization drastically reduces scope. Network segmentation, quarterly external scans (ASV), annual pen tests.
  • CCPA / CPRA (California): GDPR-lite. Right to know, right to delete, right to opt-out of sale. Less strict bases for processing; broader applicability (any business meeting size thresholds with CA residents’ data).

The unifying engineering theme: data inventory + data flow diagrams. You can’t comply with any of these if you don’t know what data you have, where it’s stored, who can access it, and where it leaves your system. Building this map early is foundational.

Right-to-be-forgotten: what makes it engineering-hard?

GDPR’s erasure right requires you to delete personal data on request. Sounds simple; isn’t.

Hard problems:

  • Backups: data lives in backups for years. Re-encrypting backups to remove a user is impractical. Solution: time-bound backups; document retention; on RTBF, accept the data is in backups but flag for deletion-on-restore. Some regulators accept this; others don’t.
  • Replicas / read-replicas / data warehouses: every downstream copy needs deletion. Without a propagation mechanism, drift is easy.
  • Audit logs: may legally need to retain (for fraud investigation) but contain PII. Solution: structure audit logs so PII is referenced (by ID), not embedded; tokenization helps.
  • Free-text fields and embedded mentions: support tickets, notes, documents may mention the user. Searching all unstructured data on RTBF is hard.
  • Third-party copies: you sent data to subprocessors (analytics, email providers); their RTBF must propagate.
  • Caches and CDNs: cache invalidation across edges.

Practical strategy: tokenize PII early so RTBF = delete-from-vault. Maintain a data flow registry so you know all destinations. Have a documented RTBF runbook. The combination of tokenization + central registry + automation is what turns RTBF from a manual project into a routine operation.

Audit logs: tamper-evident logs, hash chains, retention.

Audit logs record who did what, when, to which resource. They’re a security control (detect bad behavior) and a compliance artifact (prove processes operated).

Properties of a good audit log:

  • Append-only: writers can add but not modify or delete. Often physically separated (different account/project) so a compromised app can’t cover its tracks.
  • Tamper-evident: each entry includes a hash of the previous entry — a hash chain. Modifying an entry breaks the chain detectably. Can be extended with periodic notarization (publish chain root to public ledger or external attestation service).
  • Signed entries: each entry signed by the producing service’s key. Verifies origin even if log store is compromised.
  • Retention: compliance regimes typically require 1–7 years. Move cold logs to immutable archive (S3 Object Lock, GCS Bucket Lock).
  • Separation of duties: people who can administer the audited systems should not be able to modify the audit logs.

Modern stack: AWS CloudTrail with multi-region + organization trails + S3 Object Lock; GCP Cloud Audit Logs; or self-hosted with Vault Audit + WORM storage. Higher-end: Sigstore Rekor (transparency log for software signing) and similar transparency-log patterns adapted to internal audit.

STRIDE threat modeling: what is it, when do you do it?

STRIDE (Microsoft, Loren Kohnfelder & Praerit Garg, 1999): a checklist of threat categories to consider against each component:

  • Spoofing (identity spoofing → AuthN controls)
  • Tampering (data tampering → integrity controls, signatures)
  • Repudiation (denying actions → audit logs)
  • Information disclosure (data leak → encryption, access control)
  • Denial of service (availability → rate limits, capacity)
  • Elevation of privilege (privilege escalation → AuthZ, least privilege)

Process: draw a data-flow diagram of the system; for each component / boundary / data store, ask “what STRIDE threats apply, what mitigations exist?” Document the threats, mitigations, and accepted risks.

When: at design time for new features that touch security-sensitive data or boundaries. The cheapest time to fix architecture is before it’s built. Modern shops integrate threat modeling into design review for major changes; lightweight versions (15-minute STRIDE walkthrough on a napkin diagram) are common.

Tooling: OWASP Threat Dragon, Microsoft Threat Modeling Tool, pytm (code-driven). Or just a doc and a whiteboard. The discipline matters more than the tool.

Supply chain security: SBOMs, signed artifacts, Sigstore.

Supply-chain attacks (SolarWinds, codecov, log4shell, ua-parser-js, xz-utils) made supply chain a board-level concern. The engineering mitigations:

  • SBOM (Software Bill of Materials): a manifest of every dependency in your build. Standards: SPDX, CycloneDX. Generated at build time (Syft, Trivy, GitHub dependency graph). Lets you ask “am I affected by CVE-X?” without scanning the world.
  • Signed artifacts: containers, packages, binaries cryptographically signed by the publisher. Verification at deploy time. Cosign (the Sigstore tool) signs container images; the public Rekor transparency log records signatures.
  • SLSA (Supply-chain Levels for Software Artifacts, pronounced “salsa”): a framework defining provenance levels. SLSA-3 means signed provenance + isolated builders + non-falsifiable lineage.
  • Pinning + lockfiles: don’t resolve dependencies on every build. Pin transitive deps in a lockfile. Update deliberately, not opportunistically.
  • Reproducible builds: same source → bit-identical artifact. Lets you verify provenance independently.

Modern shops: GitHub Actions with attestation, SLSA-3 builders, Cosign signing, Rekor publishing, admission webhooks at deploy time verifying signatures. The end-to-end story: from your developer’s commit to a running container, every step is verified and recorded.

The interview signal: knowing Sigstore (Linux Foundation, 2021) and SLSA exists, and what they’re for. “We use signed images and SBOMs in CI” is a senior-level answer in 2026.
