SLOs, error budgets, alert design

SLI / SLO / SLA, burn-rate alerts, golden signals, on-call. The discipline of running services at scale; the vocabulary that signals senior.

1. SLI / SLO / SLA -- definitions and relationship

Measurement

SLI

Service Level Indicator. A quantitative measurement of service behavior. Examples: % of requests with latency < 500ms; % of requests returning 2xx.

Target

SLO

Service Level Objective. A target for the SLI. "99.9% of requests succeed." Internal engineering commitment. Always stricter than SLA.

Contract

SLA

Service Level Agreement. An SLO with business consequences if missed -- refund, credit, penalty. Customer-facing contract.

Visualizing the relationship

SLI is the chart line (actual performance). SLO is the horizontal target. SLA is the contractual cliff below which penalties apply.

The nines -- what each level costs

At 3 nines (99.9%), you have 43.2 minutes of downtime per month. This is the sweet spot for most SaaS services: achievable with multi-AZ deployment, health checks, and auto-failover. Every additional nine costs roughly 10x more engineering effort.
Takeaway: SLO is your internal target; SLA is the external contract. Always set SLO stricter than SLA -- the gap is your buffer for error. Every additional 9 costs roughly 10x more engineering effort; pick the level your users actually need, not the one that sounds impressive.

2. Error budget

Error budget = (1 - SLO) x time window. For a 99.9% SLO over 30 days:

Budget = 0.1% x (30 x 24 x 60) = 43.2 minutes of errors allowed

3. Burn rate alerts

The senior pattern from Google's SRE workbook. Naive alert ("fire if error rate > 1%") is noisy at low traffic and slow at high traffic. Multi-window burn rate fixes both.

How burn rate works

Fast burn

1h window, 14x rate

If you're burning 14x the expected rate in a 1h window, you'd exhaust your entire monthly budget in ~51 hours. Page immediately.

Slow burn

6h window, 6x rate

If you're burning 6x the expected rate over a 6h window, budget would be gone in ~5 days. Subtle degradation the fast window misses. Page within hours.

Interactive burn rate demo

Click "Simulate spike" to see a sudden error burst detected quickly by the 1h fast-burn window. Click "Simulate slow degradation" to see a gradual increase that only the 6h slow-burn window catches.
Takeaway: Multi-window burn rate captures both incidents (fast burn) and degradations (slow burn) without false positives at low traffic. Avoid pure-threshold error-rate alerts -- they're either too noisy or too slow.

5. Alert design -- antipatterns and fixes

Every alert must be: (1) actionable, (2) urgent, (3) symptomatic. If it is not all three, it is a ticket or a log line, not a page.

Bad: Single-threshold alert

"error rate > 1%" -- noisy at low QPS, 5 errors in 500 requests triggers it.

Fix: Burn rate over multi-windows. Alert on budget consumption rate, not raw error percentage.

Bad: Cause-based alert

"CPU > 80%" -- triggers on benign load spikes, batch jobs, and compaction.

Fix: Symptom-based alerting. "User-facing latency exceeds target" -- alert on what users experience.

Bad: No deduplication

100 alerts for one incident. Engineer drowns in noise while trying to respond.

Fix: Aggregate at Alertmanager level. Group by service + alert name. One page per incident.

Bad: No runbook link

Alert wakes engineer at 3am with no context. "HTTP 500 rate high." Now what?

Fix: Every alert includes link to runbook with clear "if X then Y" diagnostic steps.

Bad: Severity creep

Everything is P1. Engineers get desensitized; real P1s get lost in the noise.

Fix: 3 strict tiers: Page (wake up) / Ticket (next business day) / Log (informational). Guard the page tier.

Bad: No auto-resolution

Alert stays firing after issue clears. Dashboard stays red. Trust erodes.

Fix: Time-bound or recovery-detected resolution. Alert clears when condition clears.

Bad: Untested alerts

Alert rule written once, never validated. When the incident arrives, alert doesn't fire.

Fix: Regular fire drills. Alert validity tests. Inject synthetic failures to verify detection.

Bad: Flapping alerts

Alert constantly toggling firing/resolved/firing/resolved every few minutes.

Fix: Hysteresis -- different thresholds for fire and clear. Longer evaluation windows. Debounce.

Takeaway: Alert criteria: (1) actionable, (2) urgent, (3) symptomatic. If it is not all three, it is a ticket or a log line, not a page. The goal is that every page results in a human doing something that only a human can do, right now.

6. On-call rotation patterns

Follow-the-sun

Rotate by region: US daytime -> EU daytime -> APAC daytime. No night pages. Trade-off: harder context handoff between regions; requires 3+ geographically distributed teams.

Primary + secondary

Backup engineer for escalation if primary is unresponsive within N minutes. Reduces single-point-of-failure risk. Both carry pagers but secondary only activates on escalation.

In-hours / after-hours split

Business hours: broader team rotates. After-hours: strict on-call with explicit compensation. Reduces unnecessary night interruptions for non-critical issues.

Compensation

Pay or comp days for after-hours on-call. Not optional -- sustained unpaid on-call causes attrition. Budget for it explicitly as a cost of running a production service.

Follow-the-sun: 6 engineers, 3 regions, 7-day schedule

Takeaway: On-call is a tax on engineering time and well-being. Pay it explicitly; rotate fairly; respect off-time. Follow-the-sun eliminates night pages but requires distributed teams. Primary + secondary eliminates single-point-of-failure.

7. Incident process -- 5 stages

Every incident flows through 5 stages. The key insight: TTM (time to mitigate) is the metric that matters to users -- minimize it at all costs. Roll back before debugging. Root cause and lasting fix come after service is restored.

Stage Action Metric Goal
1. Detect Alert fires (or external report arrives) TTD -- time to detection < 5 min via monitoring
2. Triage Assess severity, page responders, assemble team TTT -- time to triage < 10 min for P1
3. Mitigate Restore service: rollback, traffic shift, scale up TTM -- time to mitigate < 30 min (user-facing)
4. Resolve Root-cause analysis and full fix deployed TTR -- time to resolve Hours to days
5. Postmortem Blameless review, action items, share-out TTP -- time to postmortem < 72 hours

Takeaway: TTM is the metric that matters to users -- minimize at all costs. Roll back before debugging. The instinct to "understand the problem first" is the enemy of fast mitigation. Restore service, then investigate.

8. Quick reference -- interview-tier answers

"How would you set SLOs?"

Start from user expectation, not engineering capability. Identify 2-3 SLIs (availability, latency, sometimes correctness/freshness). Set SLO targets that reflect what users actually need. Ratchet SLO stricter than any external SLA -- the gap is your safety margin. Review quarterly; tighten if you're always exceeding, loosen if you're burning too much engineering time defending them.

"How would you alert?"

Multi-window burn rate, not raw threshold. Symptom-based (user-facing latency/error rate), not cause-based (CPU/memory). Every alert gets a runbook link. Three severity tiers: page (wake up now), ticket (next business day), log (informational). Test alerts regularly with synthetic failures. Group by service + alert name to prevent alert storms.

"Why isn't 100% availability the goal?"

Chasing the 9s costs exponentially more. Users can't perceive the difference beyond a point -- their own ISP, device, and client-side code are less reliable than your 4+ nines. You need room for risk-taking: deploying new features, running migrations, experimenting. Error budget formalizes this: the gap between 100% and your SLO is your innovation budget.

"What's the difference between latency and saturation?"

Latency = how long this request took (RED metric, user-visible). Saturation = how much queueing exists in the system right now (USE metric, infra-visible). They're correlated but distinct: saturation is a leading indicator -- queue depth grows before latency spikes. By the time latency is bad, saturation has been bad for a while.

"Walk me through an incident process."

Five stages: Detect (TTD) -> Triage (TTT) -> Mitigate (TTM) -> Resolve (TTR) -> Postmortem (TTP). The critical insight: TTM is the user-facing metric. Roll back or shed traffic immediately; do not debug in production while users suffer. Root cause analysis comes after service is restored. Postmortem is blameless, action-item-driven, and published within 72 hours.

The senior tell. Talk in error budgets, not "is everything green?" The right vocabulary is: "we're at 30% burn this month -- we can ship with confidence" or "we're at 110% -- this PR waits." The junior says "uptime looks fine." The senior says "we've consumed 40% of our error budget with 60% of the month remaining -- we have room to ship the migration."
